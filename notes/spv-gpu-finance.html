SPVs in financial history and GPU project finance

How special-purpose vehicles (SPVs) work, why neoclouds such as CoreWeave put GPU clusters in them, and what 150 years of SPV use in other industries says about where the structure holds and where it breaks.

"The financing looks remarkably like aircraft ABS. Wall Street is financing GPU clusters through bankruptcy-remote SPVs, often using 70-80% LTV structures supported by multi-year contracts. The contracts make the cash flows financeable, but the equity highly leveraged and sensitive to assumptions."

In short: rail and aircraft finance are cheap because the law and the asset both protect the lender. GPU finance is cheap only when the contract does that job, and expensive when the lender has to rely on the GPUs.

The GPU figures come from the wiki digests and podcast pages listed at the end. The historical cases are from general financial history, and their figures are approximate. The $1B cluster used in the charts is an illustration in round numbers, not a specific deal.

The structure

Who pays whom, and who holds which rights

An SPV is a separate company that owns one asset: here, one GPU cluster plus its customer contract. Lenders lend to the SPV against that asset and contract, customer payments go into a lender-controlled account, debt service is paid first, and what remains goes up to the sponsor as equity. The sponsor usually stays on as operator under a management agreement.

The neocloud gets
  • Debt priced off the customer. About 6-7% inside a ring-fenced SPV with a good contract against about 15% on its own name.
  • Growth beyond its balance sheet. CoreWeave spent $14.1B on infrastructure in H1 2026 against $3.7B of operating cash. At 70-80% LTV, each contract largely finances its own cluster.
  • Containment. Debt is non-recourse or limited-recourse. A cancelled contract or a late site costs that SPV, not the company, and covenants apply per SPV.
The hyperscaler or lab gets
  • Capacity without owning depreciating hardware. Many deals are booked as service contracts (ASC 606) rather than leases (ASC 842), so they stay off the balance sheet. Obsolescence and residual-value risk stay with the SPV's equity and lenders.
  • Protection from the supplier failing. Customers have prepaid 15-25% of contract value. Inside a bankruptcy-remote SPV, the cluster and the prepayment sit outside the neocloud's bankruptcy estate, often with step-in rights.
  • Speed. A neocloud with land, power and a financing template delivers faster than a self-build.
Lenders get
  • One asset, one contract and a cash waterfall with no competing creditors.
  • SemiAnalysis prices the residual execution risk at about 100bp over the customer's credit.
  • When the customer isn't investment grade (OpenAI, Anthropic), a hyperscaler or Nvidia stands in the middle: rent-back floors and residual-value guarantees ("balance sheet as a service").
Section 1

What "bankruptcy-remote" means

Unlikely to fail on its own

Its charter limits it to one business with no other debt. Separateness covenants require its own books, bank accounts and name, with no commingled cash and no guarantees of the sponsor's debts. An independent director must approve any voluntary bankruptcy filing, so the sponsor can't drag it into a filing.

Unlikely to be pulled into the sponsor's bankruptcy

Assets move in by a "true sale," not a disguised loan, and law firms give non-consolidation opinions that a court would not merge the SPV with the sponsor ("substantive consolidation").

"Remote" is not "proof": see General Growth Properties in the history section.

Section 2

Same company, very different borrowing costs

The SPV moves the deal's credit from the neocloud's own (sub-investment-grade) name to the customer's contract plus the neocloud's ability to deliver it.

What it costs
  • Structural subordination. The best assets are pledged to SPV lenders, so parent-level unsecured bondholders recover less (S&P's 10-30 cents).
  • Trapped cash. Cash moves up only after debt service and coverage tests.
  • Less flexibility. Pledged GPUs can't easily move to a better-paying customer.
  • Risk delayed, not removed. When debt outlasts the contract, the lender is betting on renewal pricing, and pays for it (+5.5% vs. +2.25%).
Section 3

Where a year of customer payments goes

Illustrative $1B cluster at 75% LTV: $750M of debt at about 7%, amortizing over 5 years, costs about $183M a year. A 5-year take-or-pay contract pays $350M a year, and operating costs take $100M. Cash is paid out in a fixed order, and the sponsor's equity is last.

Why the equity is so sensitive

At 75% LTV, a $100 cluster carries $75 of debt and $25 of equity, and the equity takes losses first. A 10% fall in value cuts the equity 40%, to $15; a 25% fall wipes it out.

Contract revenue pays debt service first, so a renewal at 30% lower rent can remove most of the equity's cash in years 4-6 while lenders are still paid.

The contract makes the senior debt cheap, and the equity carries the uncertainty about renewal rents, utilization and residual value.

Railroad equipment trusts

A trustee owns the rolling stock and leases it to the railroad; investors are paid from the lease. Railroads financed equipment cheaply even when their bonds were junk. Through the receiverships of the 1890s and 1930s and Penn Central in 1970, equipment trust holders were usually paid while other bondholders lost, because a bankrupt railroad still needs its locomotives and another railroad would take them. Congress wrote this into Section 1168 (rail) and Section 1110 (aircraft).

Lesson: SPVs work best when the asset is essential to the user and easy to redeploy.

Project finance

Each project is its own company: sponsors put in equity, banks lend non-recourse, a long-term offtake contract repays the debt, and a contractor builds at a fixed price. This is the structural parent of the neocloud deals.

  • Dabhol, India (1990s-2001): an Enron-led, roughly $2.9B power plant. The state electricity board stopped paying in 2001 and the plant sat idle for years.
  • Merchant power (2002-05): gas plants financed against market prices instead of contracts. After an overbuild, Mirant, NRG and later Calpine filed, and lenders took the keys to many plant SPVs.
  • Toll roads: Sydney's Cross City Tunnel went into receivership in 2006, a year after opening, on traffic far below forecast.

Lesson: an SPV is only as good as its offtaker, and a forecast is not a contract.

Aircraft lease securitization

Lessors put leased planes into an SPV issuing tranched notes at about 65-80% of appraised value, with the lessor as servicer. GPA's Airplanes Group (1996, about $4B) was the landmark deal; after 9/11 lease rates and values fell, and the junior classes stopped being paid. In 2020, airlines deferred rent, many deals breached coverage tests, cash was locked in for the senior notes, and junior tranches were impaired.

Lesson: the waterfall protected senior lenders as designed. It could not protect against a sector-wide fall in what the asset earns.

Enron

Accounting rules then let a company keep an SPE off its balance sheet if outsiders held 3% of its equity. Enron used hundreds (Chewco, JEDI, LJM, the Raptors), run in part by CFO Andrew Fastow, to book gains, hide debt and "hedge" its investments. The Raptors were capitalized mainly with Enron stock, so the hedges failed as the stock fell. Enron restated about $586M of earnings in November 2001 and filed that December. Result: Sarbanes-Oxley and FIN 46.

Lesson: an SPV backed by the sponsor's own credit transfers no risk.

Mortgage securitization and CDOs

Originators sold mortgages into trust SPVs that issued tranched securities; CDOs bought the risky tranches and re-tranched them. The legal ring-fence mostly held. The economics failed: originators who kept no risk stopped caring about loan quality, tranching assumed defaults wouldn't happen together, and servicers couldn't modify or foreclose properly at scale.

Lesson: an SPV can isolate legal risk while spreading economic risk.

SIVs and ABCP conduits

Bank-sponsored vehicles bought long-dated securities funded with commercial paper maturing in weeks. When money funds stopped rolling the paper in August 2007, Citigroup took about $49B of SIV assets onto its balance sheet and HSBC about $45B; IKB needed a rescue. Northern Rock's Granite trust stayed legally intact, but the bank failed on its funding model. FAS 166/167 (2009) forced many vehicles back onto balance sheets.

Lesson: off-balance-sheet is not off-the-hook, and short funding for long assets fails in a run.

German KG ship funds

Each ship was its own limited partnership: retail investors bought the equity for tax benefits, regional banks provided most of the debt, and charters often ran shorter than the loans. After the 2005-08 ordering boom, the Baltic Dry index fell from about 11,800 in May 2008 to about 660 by December. As charters rolled off, the single-ship KGs defaulted in large numbers; HSH Nordbank needed a state rescue in 2009 and was sold in 2018.

Lesson: when every SPV holds the same asset, financed the same way at the same time, the losses all land on the same lenders. This is the closest analog to a GPU overbuild.

The legal test: General Growth Properties (2009)

The mall owner had financed hundreds of properties in bankruptcy-remote SPVs with independent directors. When GGP filed, it put many of those SPVs into Chapter 11 too, including performing ones, after replacing their independent directors shortly before the filing. The court let the filings stand because the SPVs were part of an integrated enterprise. Lenders were eventually paid but lost control of timing and cash.

Section 5

Where SPVs hold and where they break

What SPVs do well
  • Isolate one cash flow so lenders can price it cheaply.
  • Let builders grow beyond their balance sheets.
  • Protect senior lenders through the payment order.
  • Survive sponsor bankruptcy when the asset is essential and easy to redeploy.
What they cannot handle
  1. Customer failure or contract expiry. The ring-fence protects an asset, not its revenue (Dabhol, merchant power, ship charters).
  2. Debt longer than the contract. Lenders become bettors on market prices (merchant power, KG funds).
  3. Sector-wide repricing (ships, aircraft in 2001 and 2020, mortgages in 2007).
  4. Support from the sponsor's own credit (Enron's stock-backed hedges, SIV backstops).
  5. Short funding for long assets (SIVs, Northern Rock).
  6. Bad incentives when the originator or builder keeps no risk (originate-to-distribute mortgages).
  7. Dependence on the operator. The legal box doesn't run itself (mortgage servicers; for GPUs, a neocloud is hard to replace mid-contract).
Section 6.1

No statutory repossession for GPUs

The usual rule: the automatic stay

When a company files for Chapter 11, the automatic stay (Section 362) freezes every creditor, secured ones included. A lender holding a lien on equipment can't repossess it; the debtor can keep using the equipment while the case runs, often for many months or years. The lender can ask for "adequate protection," payments meant to offset the collateral losing value. In practice it waits, argues about valuation, and may have its claim restructured.

The aircraft and rail carve-outs

Sections 1110 (aircraft and ships) and 1168 (rail equipment) take these assets out of that rule. Within 60 days of the filing, the debtor must either agree to keep paying and cure any defaults, or hand the equipment back. If it does neither, the lender can repossess without asking the court. The Cape Town Convention gives similar rights for aircraft in many other countries.

A lender knows that within about two months it will either be paid in full or hold the asset. Lower expected losses mean lower rates and higher loan-to-value, and bankrupt airlines usually keep paying on the planes they need.

Section 6.2

Why depreciation speed matters

A secured lender's recovery is roughly the collateral's value on the day it can sell, minus the cost of seizing and reselling it. GPUs are weaker collateral than planes or locomotives on every count.

Aircraft / locomotiveGPU cluster
Useful life25+ years / decades6-year book life at CoreWeave (4 at Nebius); critics argue about 4 years of economic life
Value pathSlow, predictable decline; independent appraisers publish valuesSet by the next chip generation: Vera Rubin is about 3x Grace Blackwell at the speeds providers sell
Resale marketDeep and global; any airline can fly the planeExists while compute is scarce; would flood if many lenders sell at once in a glut
Getting it backFly it awayRacks wired into someone else's building, with power, cooling and networking; removal and resale cost real money

The two weaknesses compound. A six-month delay costs a plane lender a few percent of value; it could cost a GPU lender a much larger share. The timing is also worst when it matters: a neocloud fails because rental rates fell, which is when GPUs are worth least. H100 rents fell to about $1.30 an hour in fall 2025 before recovering to about $3. That range is why SemiAnalysis says most deals assume no residual value at all.

Section 6.3

The healthy pattern: the contract repays the debt

Classic project finance, such as a power plant with a long power purchase agreement, works because the lender rarely needs the collateral. That takes an investment-grade offtaker, and a contract whose revenue covers debt service with a margin and outlasts the debt.

What can still break a contract-backed deal
  • Termination rights. If the customer can cancel, for example because the operator misses delivery dates or service levels, the contract falls away and the lender is back to relying on GPU collateral, without Section 1110. That is the "execution risk" SemiAnalysis prices at about 100bp over the customer's own spread.
  • Construction and power delays. Interest accrues before revenue starts.
  • A thinner backstop than it looks. A hyperscaler reselling capacity to a lab may have rights to pass problems through to the lab.
Sections 6.4 and 6.5

Patterns that match past failures, and what to watch

Debt outlasting contracts

CoreWeave's $2.6B facility, about five years against three-year contracts, is the merchant-power and KG-fund setup.

Many single-site SPVs, same window, same asset

The KG-fund setup: each failure contained, all losses landing on the same lenders.

Sponsor-linked support for non-IG customers

Nvidia's backstops ($497B per SemiAnalysis Ep. 037, $588B per Patel) come from the party that benefits from the demand. This is not Enron, but it is the circularity question to watch.

The variable to watch: sector-wide renewal pricing, more than any one SPV's legal integrity. Brandon Carl's shortage-then-glut argument is the scenario where that repricing happens across every box at once.
