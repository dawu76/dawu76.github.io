The Week: Stocks Up 2.5%, the Two-Year Up 36bp

The S&P rose 2.5% to 7,561.67, but Monday's oil-driven 2.35% did nearly all of it; the rest of the week was flat through hot CPI and PPI. The index sits between its 200-day at 7,340 and a 50-day at 7,667 it failed to reclaim.

  • One day did the workS&P +2.5%, best since August; Monday +2.35% on Brent -5.8%; Mon-Fri +0.2%

    One day did the work (Michael Gayed, The Lead-Lag Report, "The S&P 500 Gained 2.5% the Week the Two-Year Yield Rose 36 Basis Points"; Lance Roberts via Adam Taggart, Thoughtful Money, "Will Struggling Nvidia Take The Markets Down With It?"). The S&P closed at 7,561.67, up 2.5%, its first weekly gain since October after four straight declines; the Nasdaq Composite rose 2.3% and the Dow 2.1%. Monday's 2.35% gain to 7,549.94 was the best day since April 8, set off by a 5.8% drop in Brent to $92.57 after Trump called renewed strikes on Iran "highly unlikely."

    Brent had started the month above $112. From Monday's close to Friday's the index added only 11.73 points, about 0.2%, through a hot CPI and PPI.

    • Rebound, not a new trend: energy (XLE) rose 5.4% but is still down 7.2% for November, SMH rose 4.9% (down 6.8% for the month) and XLK 3.4% (down 5.1%). Small caps lagged, IWM +1.4% against SPY +2.6%. The leaders were the groups hit hardest the week before.
    • Why it matters Gayed reads the four flat days as stocks not yet pricing what bonds priced. "When several markets agree and one disagrees, I give the benefit of the doubt to the several."
    • Vs. prior digests Friday's close was down about 0.4% from Thursday's 7,592.40 (Stochastic Volatility), close to Bernstein's SPY -0.38% (market-newsletter-digest-2026-11-13 §1).

Stock Cheat Sheets has the S&P down 0.84% and the Nasdaq 1.40% on Friday, against about 0.4% from the closing levels.

Nvidia After Huang: The Loop, the Bank and Tuesday's Report

Nvidia fell 12.8% for the week to $162.85. The weekend's question is the loop Huang ran: $530B of commitments against $91B of liabilities and $98.9B of stakes in other companies, now in the hands of the CFO who cut the OpenAI backstop.

  • -12.8% to $162.8532% below the $239.24 record; Kovacs's short 20% in the money

    The week in the stock (Roberts; Kovacs). Nvidia fell 12.8% for the week to $162.85, 32% below its October 6 record of $239.24. On Thursday it opened down 13%, touched $148.80 and closed down 4.3%.

    Roberts: "The market isn't repricing Nvidia's chips. It's repricing the uncertainty over who runs the company." Sam Kovacs, short since November 5, is 20% in the money.

    • Vs. prior digests Thursday's close was $166.19 (market-newsletter-digest-2026-11-13 §1), so Friday took another 2% off. Parekh's 35% drawdown was measured near the lows; 32% is the close.
  • Investor, lender, supplier, backup customer$530B from $184B; $279B supply; $105B OpenAI; $91B liabilities

    The funding loop, step by step (Reader: Damnang, quoting Bull Theory). Nvidia invests in or guarantees the loans of a customer, the customer builds a data center, fills it with Nvidia GPUs, Nvidia books the sale, and if capacity goes unrented Nvidia buys it.

    "Nvidia is the investor, the lender, the supplier and the backup customer, all in the same deal."

    The filing for the quarter to July 26 shows $530B of commitments and guarantees, up from $184B a quarter earlier: $279B of wafer and memory supply (from $119B), up to $105B guaranteed on OpenAI's Ohio site, and $36B of neocloud capacity floors, against $91B of on-balance-sheet liabilities. CoreWeave has a floor of up to $6.3B through 2032; Nvidia invested $1.5B in the company building Ohio.

    • Damnang's worry is the chain: if Kress tightens terms, lenders ask more collateral, less capacity gets built even with demand unchanged, and the next round of financing gets harder. Guarantee terms allow written amendments and some initiatives are still memoranda, so existing deals can shrink too. "How much of the AI future we have been anticipating depended on the assumption that Jensen would keep making the next big bet?"
    • Vs. prior digests the $530B total and the $279B supply figure match SemiAnalysis (market-newsletter-digest-2026-11-12 §3). The OpenAI figure was flagged as $105B (FUNDA) against $100B (Parekh) a day earlier; Bull Theory sides with $105B. See spv-history-and-gpu-project-finance.
  • The bank of the AI boom$98.9B of stakes, $23.7B of gains; Noble: revenue 30-40% short

    The bank of the AI boom (George Noble, The Noble Update, "NVIDIA's biggest bulls just showed you how scared they are"; Michael Parekh, AI-RTZ #1270; Jesse Felder, The Felder Report). At the end of July Nvidia held $98.9B of stakes in other companies, many of them customers, and booked $23.7B of gains on them in the first six months of the fiscal year. The Information counts about $140B of Nvidia dealmaking, guarantees included, in two months this year (Parekh).

    Noble expects the interim CEO and new chair to be "far more careful," neoclouds to start filing for bankruptcy, and Nvidia's revenue to land 30-40% short of what it would have been. Parekh calls the "Jensen put" paused; Bloomberg via Felder: Huang "is a consummate dealmaker."

    • Counterpoint (Daniel Romero, Hypertech Investing): Nvidia "hasn't played a massive role in funding these companies"; GPU allocation matters more, and any chip seller prefers a spread-out customer base, so the new leadership should keep backing neoclouds.
    • Why it matters Noble's and Romero's views differ on the size of the financing channel, not its direction. The $98.9B of stakes and $140B of two-month dealmaking are the numbers to test against Tuesday's disclosures.
  • Tuesday: $2.49 on ~$109BRecord $124B volume, calls at the 99th percentile: crowded, not capitulating

    What Tuesday has to show (Roberts; The Data-Driven Investor). Consensus is about $2.49 a share on revenue near $109B, a little above Nvidia's own $108B guide. Roberts wants guidance, how much of the backlog leans on customer financing, and what Kress says about the search.

    The Data-Driven Investor sees crowding, not capitulation: record dollar volume of $124B Thursday, call prices at the 99th percentile of the year, and puts sold rather than bought. It expects the stock to fall modestly or "pop and drop," then get sold again as money moves to Anthropic.

    • ConflictRoberts says Huang had been asked to appear before three House committees on November 18 and calls it unclear whether that happens; Bernstein had a December 1 deadline to agree to testify (market-newsletter-digest-2026-11-13 §5). The November 18 date may be the original request.
    • Different measureNvidia rose only 28% in the 12 months into its high, so the 2000-style excess sits in memory and AMD more than Nvidia (Data-Driven). After its first close 30% below a high, Nvidia went much lower three times in five (2008, 2018, 2022).
    • Vs. prior digests retail bought $111M in the first 30 minutes Thursday, a record (market-newsletter-digest-2026-11-13 §2). Record dollar volume and rich calls confirm the marginal holder is long into the print.
  • Retail GPU prices crackRTX 5090 -¥240,000 in Japan; Newegg at 3.13-3.68x MSRP

    Retail GPU prices crack (Reader: Donald J. Gorbachev). Seventy-two hours after the resignation, RTX 5090 street prices in Japan fell ¥240,000 (about $1,500) and RTX 5080s ¥60,000, and Newegg relisted first-party 5090s at $6,249.99 to $7,349.99, 3.13-3.68 times the $1,999 MSRP. At $6,250 a 5090 gives 5.1 GB of VRAM and 134 FP8 TFLOPS per $1,000, close to a $35,000 B200's 5.1 GB and 129 TFLOPS.

    Gorbachev's comparison: four Tenstorrent Blackhole p150a cards for $5,596 give 128 GB pooled, 4.5x the memory per dollar.

    • Caveat: this is a polemic built on one week of retail prices; a price drop in Japan and relisting in the US are signs of loosening supply, not proof of the "gray-market pipe freezing up" the author claims.

Theme 4

Washington, China and the Export Fight

Gavin Baker argues selling China weaker chips widened the US compute lead from 2.8x to 8.8x and that a full ban would push Chinese labs onto Huawei. Taipology expects tighter transshipment controls that Xi will welcome.

  • Baker: the current policy workedFLOPs ratio 2.8x to 8.8x, 14.1x modeled; 600,000 H200s held in Hong Kong

    The case for selling China weaker chips (Reader: Gavin Baker). Baker argues both administrations' policy of exporting less powerful GPUs while keeping the best has worked: the US-to-China AI FLOPs ratio rose from 2.8x in 2023 to 8.8x in 2026 and is modeled at 14.1x in 2027, figures he attributes to Grok and Claude. Chinese labs prefer American GPUs to Huawei's, which starves Huawei of the heavy use that improves chips, and labs renting US hyperscaler capacity in Southeast Asia can be cut off in a conflict.

    A full ban would push them onto Huawei's power-hungry optical scale-up systems; China "has a lot of power" and could share that stack with Russia, North Korea or Iran.

    • Caveat: the FLOPs ratios are model-generated estimates, not a published dataset. Baker is also an Nvidia holder who co-signed Friday's letter to the board (§3).
    • Vs. prior digests Baker cites the roughly 600,000 H200s Beijing is keeping in Hong Kong warehouses: "China Hawks should consider why Beijing agrees with them." The same figure, worth $20-30B, was in market-newsletter-digest-2026-11-13 §5. See china-ai-compute-access-export-controls-2026.
  • Xi gets his wishTighter transshipment; WSJ: "Maybe that was China's plan"

    What tightening means (Taipology). Oung expects transshipment controls on Nvidia chips to tighten, which pleases US labs and hurts Chinese ones already short of chips. "But guess who's not going to be mad? Xi Jinping," who wants labs on domestic chips; with a more hawkish Nvidia leadership, "Xi will get his wish without having to be the bad guy."

    The Wall Street Journal's editorial: the resignation "was necessary, but it also does regrettable harm to the company and America's AI industry. Maybe that was China's plan."

    • Vs. prior digests Sun Chenghao's decoupling loop in the 11-13 digest makes the same point from Beijing's side.
  • A conspiracy readingWalsh: motive, no evidence

    A conspiracy reading (Reader: Paul Walsh). Walsh asks whether Huang was removed because he threatened US labs and intelligence agencies by selling to China and backing open-source models, rather than national security. He offers no evidence beyond motive: "We can't know anything for sure yet."

    • Caveat: speculation, kept because it shows how far the open-source angle has spread. Oung raises the same motive more carefully (§3).

  • SOX tracks 2000+179% run-up; -30.7% on day 102 vs. -30.9%; Cisco bounced 22%, then -64%

    The SOX looks like 2000 so far (The Data-Driven Investor). The semiconductor index closed at 10,146, 30.7% below its June 22 high, after a 179% run in the 12 months into that high; only 2000 (+264%) was bigger. On trading day 102 after the peak it is down 30.7%; on day 102 after March 2000 it was down 30.9%.

    Of six earlier 30% drawdowns, four fell another 23-81%; the two that recovered within months (March 2020, April 2025) were macro shocks after smaller run-ups. All three cases with a run-up above 130% ended 52-87% below the peak.

    • Bounces: SOX rose about 20% off the November 5 low and gave back more than a third by Friday. In 2000 it rallied 30% in the 60 days after its first -30% close; Cisco bounced 22% in 20 days and was 64% lower a year later.
    • Why it matters for the index: semis used to fall 25% with the S&P near its highs when they were a few percent of it. The one comparable case by weight, August 2000, saw the S&P fall 10% within 60 days; with SOX below its 200-day and the S&P above, S&P 20-day returns averaged +0.23% against +0.80%.
    • Different measureDaniel Oliver has SOXX 33% below its June high (Alethea) and Roberts has semis 31% below; all three are close.
  • Leaders down, index up41 of 50 biggest gainers in bear markets; Livermore and Grantham

    Leaders down, index up (Sam Kovacs, Babylon Burns, "The Last Stage Of A Bubble Looks Exactly Like This"). The S&P is 3% below its 7,819 record, but the top 20 tech names, 41.3% of the index, are down 9.8% as a group since the peak while the top 30 non-tech names, 21%, are up 9.4%. Of the 50 biggest gainers since October 2022, 41 (80%) are in bear markets, against 96% in April 2025, when the index had fallen 19%.

    Kovacs cites Livermore (leaders falling one after another and, "for the first time in months, did not come back") and Grantham's finding that in 1929 and 2000 speculative stocks fell about 30% before the index broke while a shrinking group of blue chips kept rising.

    • Why it matters the blue-chip leg (Berkshire, Lilly, JPMorgan, Visa, Walmart, Exxon) is what is holding the index up now, and Kovacs is long that leg and short the laggards.

  • $5.4T in, $7.4T outInterest ~$1.1T; debt $41T to $46T by 2030

    The FY2026 numbers (John Mauldin, Thoughts from the Frontline, "Who Pays the Government's Bills?"). Washington took in $5.4T and spent $7.4T in fiscal 2026, a deficit just under $2T, about 6.2% of GDP. Net interest crossed $1T for the first time, about $1.1T, more than defense.

    Debt held by the public is about 100% of GDP ($33T); total debt is about 123% ($41T), heading to $46T by 2030 under current law per CBO. Mauldin's target is a deficit below nominal GDP growth, a gap of about $1T a year: "we are rounding the turn and entering the home stretch of what will become a major sovereign debt crisis."

    • Receipts: individual income tax $2.85T (53%), payroll $1.81T (34%), corporate $381B (7%), customs $173B net ($318B gross less about $145B of refunds after the Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariffs in February), excise $106B, estate and gift $39B.
    • Why it matters for rates: a 6.2% deficit at full employment and $1.1T of interest is the supply Bassman and Knapp blame for sticky long yields (§8).
  • Who paysTop 1% 38.4%; bottom half 3.3%; rates a poor guide to revenue

    Who pays (Mauldin). In 2023, 153.1M returns reported $15.2T of income and paid $2.14T, an average rate of 14.1%. The top 1% paid 38.4% of income taxes, against 19.3% in 1980 when the top rate was 70%; about 1,500 returns in the top 0.001% paid $75B (3.5%) at an average rate of 23.6%, lower than the slices below because their income is mostly capital gains.

    • Mauldin's conclusion: "Statutory rates are a poor guide to revenue," so closing a $1T gap with "tax the rich" alone runs into mobile, deferrable capital income. His next letter will test it.
  • Transfers prop up counties17.6% of income vs. ~8% in 1970; Owsley County 63%

    Transfers prop up local economies (Reader: Nikhil Krishnan). Government transfers are 17.6% of US personal income, up from about 8% in 1970, and Medicare and Medicaid are 46% of transfer dollars. 53% of counties, about 72M people, get more than 25% of income from transfers; Owsley County, Kentucky gets 63%, with Medicaid alone 32%.

    New York's paid family-caregiver program added 57,000 jobs in 2023, 38% of the state's job growth. Krishnan thinks the bargain of healthcare jobs funded by federal dollars "is falling apart."

    • Vs. prior digests CBO, via Mauldin, has Medicaid and CHIP worth about 48% of the lowest fifth's market income. Knapp lists elevated transfers among the drags on labor-market churn (§8), and Hobart rejects the claim that Medicaid subsidizes employers like Walmart.

    Where the excess return comes from (Adam Tooze, Chartbook). Brad Setser: all of the "excess return" behind the US income surplus, the exorbitant privilege, comes from profits booked in a few tax havens. Tooze also flags record US gold exports.
