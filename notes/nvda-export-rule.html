1. The rules

Issued Monday, November 16: BIS interim final rule, "Addressing Remote Access to Advanced Semiconductors" (FR Doc. 2026-23529; rule text, BIS release). It is scheduled for publication and takes effect on November 19, with comments due 60 days later. BIS says it was "written by and at the direction of" Treasury and the National Security Council. The 90-day window for existing data centers therefore ends around February 17, 2027. Shipments already en route on November 19 have 30 days to arrive under the old terms. 1.1 Who needs a license, what US companies can still do, and what gets reported

The rule applies two separate tests. The five-country license asks where the chips physically ship, and that is the only test the US-company exemption waives. The remote-access control asks who uses the compute, and it applies to every company in every country, US hyperscalers included.

Both tests attach to the chips, not to the rental. The EAR controls exports, re-exports and in-country transfers of items; renting compute is not itself an export. The rule reaches rentals in two ways. A supplier that knows a data center will serve Chinese firms above the threshold needs a license to ship to it. And if a data center starts serving them after delivery, the EAR treats a change of end use or end user inside a foreign country as a "transfer (in-country)" (§ 734.16), which § 744.24(e) says needs BIS authorization. That indirect route is why Nvidia, Baker McKenzie and others argue Commerce needs RASA to regulate cloud access directly (§6). This section is my reading of the rule text, not legal advice.

The 12M threshold is smaller than it looks for a hyperscaler:

  • Linked sites count as one. It is summed across "linked data centers": sites networked so they "share resources, balance workloads, and replicate data as a unified whole," including sites joined only digitally. Hyperscaler regions replicate data and fail over to each other, so read literally much of a hyperscaler's network could be one pool. BIS hasn't said how far that goes. The test is the network, not the country: a Johor site linked to a US site would count the US capacity too. A ChinaTalk commenter points this out.
  • Chinese customers are added together. The text counts access "to or for companies headquartered in" China, Macau or D:5, which reads as the total across all such customers, not per customer.
  • So any meaningful AI rental to Chinese firms needs a license. The policy for China-headquartered firms elsewhere in the rule is presumption of denial.

How it's enforced:

  • Customer checks. Note 3 to § 744.24 says a supplier keeps its "no knowledge" defense only if it can get the data center's "know your customer" checks, including whether any customer is directly or indirectly owned or controlled from China, Macau or D:5. A data center that refuses or can't provide them is a red flag that "presumptively" gives the supplier knowledge.
  • Notice passes down the chain. § 744.24(e) requires anyone who ships without a license (because it had no knowledge of a prohibited use) to tell the recipient in writing that serving Chinese firms above the threshold needs BIS authorization, and that the recipient must follow BIS's know-your-customer guidance (Supplement No. 3 to Part 732). The data center operator then can't claim it didn't know the rule applied.
  • Later holders are covered. Note 1 to § 744.24(a)(1) applies the prohibition to whoever holds the chips when they are re-exported or transferred, whether or not that party was in the original export.

The monthly reports (§ 743.10). They are filed by the exporter, re-exporter or transferor (Nvidia, Dell, Supermicro or the ODM), not by the hyperscaler that receives the chips. Each report covers:

  • Every party: name, physical and mailing address, phone, and role (exporter, consignee, end user, ultimate parent). The same details for every parent company's headquarters, with the ultimate parent identified.
  • Destination: where the chips end up, and any end users other than the consignee.
  • Each product: ECCN at paragraph level (e.g. 3A090.a), product name and SKU, TPP, and quantity.
  • Date of each shipment or transfer.
  • Logistics: submitted at bis.gov/ai/exclusionnotesubmission, due by the first business day of the month after the shipment, covering shipments from November 19.

The reports track hardware, not tenants: nothing in them says who uses the compute. Tenant use is policed through the § 744.24 threshold and the customer checks. What the reports do give BIS is a monthly count of chips at each site, which it can compare with the capacity the site sells. That kind of comparison could have flagged a Megaspeed-style operator, whose chip count far outran its visible customer base. Megaspeed itself wasn't US-owned, so it would have needed licenses rather than reports (nvidia-gpu-sales-chain-southeast-asia-megaspeed-2026).

Chances that five-country licenses aren't issued. The review standard is case by case, weighing "the effectiveness of a destination's export control regimes and coordination with BIS," not presumption of denial. The rule removes the Data Center Validated End User (VEU) route, so there is no blanket pre-approval and each non-US buyer applies deal by deal. BIS expects only ~100 more applications a year, which implies it expects most volume to flow through the US-company exemption. The odds below are my judgment, not anything the rule states.

Investor takeaway: the rule is a licensing regime, not a ban. US hyperscalers keep shipping into Southeast Asia license-free, but nobody, US or local, can rent meaningful Nvidia capacity to Chinese firms abroad without BIS approval. For Nvidia that means delays for non-US local buyers in the five countries and a near cutoff for Chinese-linked operators, not blanket denial. §4.1 counts the expected delays in the most likely case, and the pessimistic case carries about $15B of them.

1.2 Enforcement and politics

From a ChinaTalk discussion the day the rule was issued, with Chris McGuire (CFR, former NSC) and Julian Gewirtz (Columbia, former NSC senior director for China), hosted by Jordan Schneider. These are former Biden officials who favor tight controls; treat their numbers as advocates' claims.

  • Commerce's credibility. The press release crediting Treasury and the NSC signals, in Gewirtz's reading, that the administration knows Commerce is "low on credibility." McGuire: Commerce still has to enforce it, and "I'm not convinced the current leadership can make that happen." He says Commerce knew about the Megaspeed activity for months.
  • No large penalties yet. McGuire contrasts BNP Paribas's ~$9B sanctions fine, which changed bank behavior, with a ~$500M Applied Materials fine for selling banned tools to China, "a drop in the bucket" against its China sales. He says no chip company has been fined for the smuggling of the past few years, which he puts at tens of billions of dollars.
  • Trump's China trip. Gewirtz's base case: Beijing will try to "bet on Trump" in the Shenzhen meetings to weaken the rule. Because it is a licensing regime, Beijing may push to blunt it in implementation rather than retaliate. A senior official told reporters "we don't negotiate on export controls."
  • Beijing's leverage. McGuire says China is keeping "hundreds of thousands" of licensed H200s in Hong Kong port partly to press for Blackwell approval, the chips Chinese firms were getting through rented capacity.
  • Follow-up wanted. McGuire wants a rule covering US data centers "in the next month or two," and a worldwide requirement for visibility into any purchase of "hundreds of millions of dollars" of AI chips. He expects smuggling to move to countries not on the list, naming Bhutan and Nepal.
  • Public evidence of Blackwell use. A November 12 forensic report reads the GPU that created a model's starting weights from the published weights. Six models from DeepSeek, Moonshot, MiniMax and Ant Group released since April 2026 read as Blackwell; their earlier models read as Hopper. The method matched all 24 models with disclosed hardware, and a second researcher reproduced it. It shows access, not where the chips were or how they were obtained (china-ai-compute-access-export-controls-2026 §2.5). For Nvidia, it gives BIS and Congress named models to cite, which supports the stricter bills in §6 and enforcement on past sales. Paul Triolo places only DeepSeek's Blackwells inside China, supplied by Century Litong. He places the rest in rented offshore capacity, including 8,192 Blackwells in Thailand supplied by Alibaba for Kimi K3, per his sources.
  • The skeptic's view. Paul Triolo (AIStackDecrypted, Nov 17) is a critic of the controls, and his view is the opposite of McGuire's.
    • He expects Beijing to read the rule and the NDAA bills as "serious violations of the Busan truce" and to retaliate. That raises the odds of the §6.6 retaliation tail, which I hadn't tied to the November rule.
    • He argues the offshore channel keeps Chinese labs on Nvidia and CUDA, so closing it speeds Huawei Ascend, CXMT HBM, Huali's 7nm GPUs and DeepSeek's Ascend software ports. For Nvidia that is a long-run China market risk more than an FY2028 one, since direct China data-center sales are already near zero (§2).
    • His H200 figures match §4: Reuters counts ~600K in Hong Kong, with major internet firms receiving only ~10K each.
    • Details are on china-ai-compute-access-export-controls-2026 (§2.5, §8) and nvidia-gpu-sales-chain-southeast-asia-megaspeed-2026 (§4, §5).

Investor takeaway: the rule's effect on Nvidia depends on how it is run more than on its text. Weak enforcement or a Trump-Xi deal that loosens licensing would shrink the channel C loss. The § 744.24 grants in §4.1 are the place that shows up. Strict enforcement would mostly add legal risk from past sales, since BIS now says supplying data centers that mainly served Chinese firms was already covered.

Notes from the filings:

  • Direct China data-center sales are near zero. H200s go out under licenses, through a US inspection and with a 25% tariff that Nvidia hasn't passed on. Beijing has restricted purchases. Nvidia took a $0.4B H200 inventory charge in the first half and a $4.5B H20 charge in Q1 FY26.
  • So what is the ~$8B a quarter of China-HQ revenue? It's mostly not frontier data-center GPUs. It likely includes workstation and gaming GPUs. Reuters reported over 3 million RTX PRO 5500s ordered by ByteDance, Alibaba and Tencent, worth over $35B, designed to sit below the export thresholds. It may also include Hong Kong-headquartered system makers. The filing doesn't break it out.
  • Taiwan HQ is the ODMs. Nvidia estimates 76% of FY2026 Data Center revenue from Taiwan-HQ customers went to end customers in the US and Europe. Dolphin Research cites 86% for the latest quarter. The remaining 14-24%, about $4-6B a quarter at Q2's level, goes elsewhere, which includes the Gulf, Japan, Korea and Southeast Asia.
  • The remote-access exposure is split across three revenue lines. Nvidia books a sale under the headquarters of whoever it invoices, so a rack rented by a Chinese lab in Johor can be US-HQ (through Supermicro or Dell), Taiwan-HQ (through an ODM) or "Other" (sold directly, such as DGX systems). Only the Taiwan line has any end-customer estimate. That's why the remote-access estimates in §4 come from modeled rental demand, not from any filing line. The sales chain, the vendors that supply Southeast Asian clouds, why Nvidia still knows the end customer in the Megaspeed case, and who in the chain is legally exposed are on nvidia-gpu-sales-chain-southeast-asia-megaspeed-2026.
  • Profitability. Q2 gross margin was about 75% (gross profit $72.1B), operating margin 66%, and the tax rate about 16.5%. Diluted shares were 24.29 billion.

Investor takeaway: the filings can't size the remote-access channel. Direct China data-center sales are near zero, and the ~$8B a quarter of China-HQ revenue is mostly products below the thresholds, which the rule doesn't touch. Every remote-access figure on this page is therefore a model, and management disclosure would narrow the range more than any analyst note.

The $30B counts all of IFP's new Chinese remote-access capacity, including the part on US clouds abroad. That's consistent with the rule text, which gives US clouds no exemption from the remote-access control (§1).

Two adjustments to the most likely case, which roughly cancel:

  • Delays for non-US local buyers (adds loss). §1.1 gives established non-US buyers in the five countries a ~55% chance of 2-6 month licensing delays. I assume about $12.5B a year of Nvidia revenue goes to them (Singtel, YTL, Indosat, local neoclouds, and Japanese and Korean firms building there); the filings don't show it. A ~4-month delay at 55% defers ~$2.3B. Nvidia is supply-limited, so ~60% is resold or ships later in the year, leaving ~$0.9B net: $0.9B × 75% × 0.835 ÷ 24.29 ≈ −$0.02.
  • Some rental licenses granted (removes loss). § 744.24 licenses are reviewed case by case, not under a presumption of denial. If ~15% of the Chinese rental capacity gets licensed, for example to US clouds with conditions, gross falls ~$4.5B and net ~$1.8B (40% unresold): ≈ +$0.05.
  • Net about +$0.02, inside the rounding of the −$0.35, so I leave the totals unchanged. The pessimistic case already carries ~$15B of delays and assumes no licenses are granted.

4.2 How much gets resold

Nvidia is supply-limited (CoWoS packaging, HBM, Rubin ramp), so a GPU one buyer can't take usually goes to another. New Street assumes about 60% gets absorbed. Two things argue for less in 2027:

  • Power and shells are the US bottleneck. Texas has frozen ERCOT's large-load queue and state permits, ballot measures restricted data centers in about 40 towns, and turbine lead times run two years (midterms-data-center-capex-sizing-2026-11, permitting-reform-lame-duck-baaja-2026-11).
  • Funding for the buyers who would absorb it is tighter. CoreWeave debt yields over 15%, Nebius fell ~30% in three sessions, and UBS has already cut CY2028.

One argument for more: capacity built in Johor for Chinese tenants already has power. It can be re-let to US labs without waiting for US grid hookups. McGuire says several AI labs called him the morning of the rule asking which operators were affected so they could take the compute, and that "export controls have not negatively impacted a single Nvidia sale so far." That's an advocate's claim, but it fits a supply-limited market. It makes 60% look reasonable for channel C, and it's why I don't go below 30% in the pessimistic case.

I use 60% for most likely and 30% for pessimistic.

5. Consequences

5.1 Projections

  • Near term (Nov 17 report). Q4 guidance is the test: consensus is ~$124-125B, UBS ~$130B. A guide that explicitly excludes or reserves for five-country shipments would make the rule look manageable. A wide range or pulled guidance would push the market toward the pessimistic case.
  • FY2028 consensus. I expect a 1-3% revenue trim in the most likely case. That's smaller than the 25% upward revision since June, so the estimate trend would still be up. The pessimistic case would erase about a third of that revision.
  • Long term. The bigger cost is TAM, not FY2028. Chinese labs move faster onto Huawei and domestic chips, and the ~4 GW of overseas Chinese capacity SemiAnalysis expects by 2029 either goes non-Nvidia or doesn't get built. Gavin Baker's argument applies here: a full cutoff removes US leverage and grows the domestic Chinese ecosystem. [digest 2026-11-15]

5.2 Multiple

  • The forward multiple has compressed from 20.8x (end of July) to 16.9x (October 30) to ~9.8x now. UBS uses 12x, Pelosky had about 10x, and QTR says "a distressed multiple of 10x or lower might be a different conversation." [digest 2026-11-15]

It is also pricing doubts about whether FY2028 EPS holds, through the $530B of guarantees, customer financing and post-Huang execution. The China-linked revenue the rule touches is the part of the business with the weakest disclosure, so the rule adds to that doubt beyond its EPS effect.

6.3 How the scenario model works

Everything below is fiscal 2028 (February 2027 to January 2028) and is added on top of the November 16 rule's most likely case in §4: $13.6B of net revenue lost and −$0.35 of EPS. For each bill, the likely case is the Senate text, which is what conferees are working from. The pessimistic case is the House text, used as hard as it allows. Each figure takes three steps:

  • Step 1, gross revenue touched: the sales the law stops or delays.
  • Step 2, net revenue lost = gross × (1 − share resold to other buyers).
  • Step 3, EPS lost = net × gross margin × (1 − 16.5% tax) ÷ 24.29B diluted shares. Operating costs are treated as fixed, so lost gross profit falls straight to pre-tax profit.

Each $1B of net revenue lost is worth about $0.026 of EPS at a 75% gross margin, $0.022 at 65% and $0.017 at 50%. The margins differ by product, and these are my assumptions:

  • H200s to China: ~50%. Nvidia's usual ~75%, less the 25% revenue share it pays the US government on these sales, if that is booked as a cost.
  • China-specific RTX PRO-class server cards: ~65%. GDDR7 cards sell for far less than Blackwell systems.
  • Blackwell and Rubin systems: ~75%.

6.4 AI OVERWATCH: likely and pessimistic

Likely: the Senate form, about −$0.02. It writes the January H200 rule into law, extends the ban on the most advanced chips to countries of concern for 18 months, bans blanket approvals, and requires Commerce to certify each license.

  • Channel A (H200s to China). §4's most likely case already counts the ~$2B as lost, so codifying the rule adds little. What the Senate text takes away is the option to ship the ~600K H200s sitting in Hong Kong (~$15-18B), which consensus likely doesn't include. I count $1B of net revenue for licenses that would have been granted and now are slowed or denied by certification.
  • Channel B (China-HQ products below the thresholds). Untouched. The Senate text applies to chips that already need licenses, and RTX PRO-class parts designed under the thresholds don't.
  • Calculation: $1B × 50% × 0.835 ÷ 24.29 = −$0.02.

Pessimistic: the House form, about −$0.34.

  • Channel B goes to §4's pessimistic level. China-HQ revenue runs about $31.5B a year (Q2 FY27's $7.88B × 4). I assume two-thirds of it, ~$20B, is data-center hardware caught by the bandwidth prong: data-center RTX PRO 6000 cards run about 1.8 TB/s against the 1,400 GB/s line. §4's most likely case already has $2B of channel B lost, so this adds $18B gross.
  • Resale of 15%. These are China-specific models. Resale would mean moving GDDR7 and wafer supply to other products, which takes quarters.
  • Calculation: $18B × (1 − 15%) = $15.3B net × 65% × 0.835 ÷ 24.29 = −$0.34. At 30% resale the loss is −$0.28.
  • Channel A. The House text terminates existing licenses on enactment. Ending H200 China sales is already in the base. A write-down of H200 inventory is possible; Nvidia took $0.4B in the first half. That would be a one-time charge, outside the run-rate.
  • Gross margin percentage. Losing 65%-margin revenue lifts the blended margin by about 0.2 point (75.0% to 75.2%), while gross profit falls by about $10B.
  • Timing. License termination takes effect on enactment, so a January 2027 law covers all of FY2028.
  • The biggest unknown is whether the RTX PRO 5500-class parts in Reuters' $35B order sit below 1,400 GB/s. If they do, only 6000-class cards are caught, and the addition falls to perhaps $8B gross, about −$0.15. I haven't found their spec.

6.5 RASA: likely and pessimistic

Likely: the Senate text. No change in FY2028 revenue; about −$0.03 in expectation. It covers only China-linked "foreign persons of concern," only cloud rental (IaaS), and only uses with a "demonstrated" serious risk, and it expires after 10 years.

  • Revenue. The November 16 rule already requires licenses for chips going to data centers that provide this access. Violations already carry the Export Control Reform Act's penalties: civil fines of up to about $374K per violation (inflation-adjusted) or twice the deal's value, and criminal penalties of up to $1M and 20 years for willful violations. RASA's addition is clear authority over the access itself, so the rule no longer has to work through the chips.
  • It removes an upside case. Without a statute, a court could narrow the rule (Baker McKenzie's point that Commerce lacks clear authority), or a trade deal with Beijing could loosen it.
    • I give that a 20% chance by FY2028, recovering half of channel C's net loss: $30B × 40% unresold = $12B; half is $6B.
    • The expected value is $1.2B of net revenue, about −$0.03.
  • Pressure did this work before the rule did. The FT reported on November 4 that Oracle cancelled Tencent's ~$7B, five-year lease of about 100,000 AI chips in Southeast Asian data centers after US officials raised concerns, before any rule required it. A lease like that now needs a § 744.24 license (The Next Web, Network World).

Pessimistic: the House text, with Commerce using it to reach data centers inside the US. About −$0.03 in FY2028 and −$0.06 a year after.

  • What's left for RASA to reach. The November 16 rule already covers US clouds' data centers abroad. There is no US-company exemption from § 744.24, so Oracle's Tencent-type leases in Southeast Asia already need licenses and are counted in channel C. What the rule can't reach is Chinese-parented companies renting chips in data centers on US soil, because it controls exports. House RASA would let Commerce license that access.
  • Size: $5B a year of Nvidia revenue, my assumption. That's about 125,000 GPUs, or ~0.3 GW. I know of no reported Chinese rental of that size inside the US. US clouds already screen Chinese tenants under the January KYC conditions and after the Tencent cancellation, so I size this well below the overseas business.
  • Resale of 50%. US capacity is the easiest in the world to re-rent, so the realistic figure is higher. 50% is the pessimistic setting.
  • Timing. Commerce has to write a new rule, then allow a transition like the November rule's 90 days. I assume it covers half of FY2028.
  • Calculation: $5B × 50% × ½ year = $1.25B net × 75% × 0.835 ÷ 24.29 = −$0.03. The full-year run-rate from FY2029 is −$0.06.
  • Who pays. Mostly the US clouds and neoclouds holding the contracts.

6.6 MATCH: no direct revenue effect, with a retaliation tail

The MATCH Act (Ricketts) pushes allies, mainly the Netherlands and Japan, to adopt countrywide controls on chipmaking tools sold to China, including older DUV lithography, and on servicing tools already installed there. It targets ASML, Tokyo Electron, Applied Materials, Lam and KLA (§7). Nothing in it applies to GPUs or to Nvidia's supply from TSMC.

Likely: about $0.00 in FY2028.

  • Tools take years to become chips. Slowing SMIC's and Huawei's capacity growth changes Chinese GPU supply in 2028-30, not in FY2028.
  • Extra Chinese demand has nowhere to go yet. Any demand that shifts back to Nvidia has to go through licensed H200s, which Beijing restricts, or products below the thresholds, which already sell freely.
  • Long-term upside, not in my numbers. A slower Huawei ramp keeps Chinese demand open to Nvidia for longer, where licensing allows.

Pessimistic: Beijing retaliates against Nvidia, about −$0.28 if it happens.

  • The tools exist. China has restricted H200 purchases, and in 2025 it opened an antitrust finding over Nvidia's Mellanox deal and reportedly told its tech firms to stop buying China-specific RTX PRO cards. MATCH would be a new provocation aimed at China's chipmaking.
  • Size, my assumption: retaliation cuts half of China-HQ revenue (~$31.5B a year), ~$15B gross. Resale is 15%, as in §6.4, since these are China-specific products. $15B × 85% = $12.75B net × 65% × 0.835 ÷ 24.29 ≈ −$0.28.
  • Probability: I put retaliation of that size at ~10% if MATCH passes. With ~65% odds of passage by end-2027, the expected cost is about −$0.02.
  • Overlap with House OVERWATCH. Both hit channel B, and the same revenue can't be lost twice. In the both-pessimistic case channel B is already cut, so retaliation adds little there.

Without line D, the stacked strict case tops out near $110B (~16%).

The filings cap line D. In Q2 FY2027:

  • China-HQ was 8.2% of revenue.
  • Taiwan-HQ was 28%, and 76-86% of it went to US and European end customers, leaving about 4-7% of revenue for the Gulf, Japan, Korea and Southeast Asia.
  • Other was about 1%.

If every one of those dollars served Chinese users, the total would be ~14-16%. Reaching 25% needs about 9 more points, ~$60B a year, from US-HQ sales (Supermicro, Dell, direct DGX) ending up with Chinese renters abroad. The largest documented case, the Supermicro co-founder indictment, covers $2.5B of servers, $510M allegedly smuggled (§7). Nothing public supports a channel near $60B.

Gross is not net. A $173B gross hit becomes a $173B loss only if no one else buys the chips. Nvidia is supply-limited, UBS has 2027 hyperscaler capex up 69% to $1.76T, and labs moved to claim freed capacity the day the rule came out (§4.2). Same arithmetic as §6.3:

A 25% net loss needs near-zero resale, which only happens if non-Chinese demand is falling at the same time. That's a demand downturn, not export policy.

Odds (my judgment). The strict pieces are correlated: a second scandal could bring the House texts, a US-soil rule and Beijing's retaliation together. But each is a minority outcome. §6.2 puts the House OVERWATCH text at ~30% and a US-soil rule at ~20% by end-2027, and the filings contradict Culper's share.

  • ~25% of revenue touched (gross): about 5% at most.
  • ~25% of revenue lost (net): about 1-2%.

Two framings under which "25%" holds up better:

  • Lost opportunity, not lost revenue. If China were fully open, Chinese demand might be 20-25% of a larger total market. Consensus already excludes it, so losing it doesn't cut estimates.
  • A different mechanism. Chinese retaliation on supply (rare earths, gallium, packaging or optics inputs) could cut revenue more than any demand rule. That isn't an export-control scenario, and nothing on this page models it.

Investor takeaway: the 25% figure is a gross, worst-case stack that depends on a short seller's China share the filings argue against. Without it, strict laws reach ~16% gross, and resale in a supply-limited market brings the net loss back toward the pessimistic case's 7-10%. That range is the realistic worst case from export policy.

8. What would change the estimate

  • Q4 guidance and commentary (Nov 17): whether management sizes Chinese remote-access demand or the five-country exposure, and whether it repeats the 70% growth outlook.
  • Licensing in the 90 days (to about February 17): how many § 744.24 licenses BIS grants, against its own estimate of about 100 more applications a year, and whether US clouds apply for Chinese tenants or drop them as Oracle dropped Tencent. The rule text settles the earlier questions: ownership look-through runs to the "ultimate parent," and intermediaries are covered by the "for an entity" clause.
  • The final NDAA text (§6): whether the Senate or House version of AI OVERWATCH survives (only the House text reaches channel B), and whether RASA is in it at all. RASA gives Commerce authority; watch whether Commerce then uses the House text to reach data centers inside the US.
  • The Trump-Xi meetings in Shenzhen: whether licensing gets loosened in practice, or whether Beijing retaliates for what Triolo expects it to call a Busan truce violation (§6.6). That shrinks channel C without any change to the rule (§1.2).
  • Enforcement on past sales: voluntary self-disclosures, fines or Entity List action under BIS's reading that supplying data centers mainly serving Chinese firms was already covered (§1 table). This is legal risk for Nvidia and the server makers, not revenue. A case would be easier to build if investigators link the Blackwell fingerprints in Chinese models (§1.2) to specific data centers' tenant records.
  • Threshold changes on RTX PRO-class parts (channel B, the largest swing between my cases).
  • Taiwan-HQ end-customer share in the next 10-Q. A fall from 76-86% US/Europe would show diverted demand being replaced.
  • Hyperscaler capex guides for 2027 (UBS: +69% to $1.76T), which set how much displaced supply gets absorbed.

Investor takeaway: three near-term data points decide which case plays out. First, the Q4 guide: does it reserve for five-country slippage or size China exposure? Second, how many § 744.24 licenses BIS grants before mid-February. Third, whether the final NDAA carries the House OVERWATCH bandwidth test. Until then, the most likely case is the one to hold.
