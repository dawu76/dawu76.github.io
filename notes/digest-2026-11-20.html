The Tape: A Seventh Loss Ends in a Capitulation-Style Close

Investor takeaway: Expect a bounce, but don't treat it as the low. The S&P closed near 6,910, down about 3.7% and 11.62% below its record, with the VIX between about 35 and 40, which looks like capitulation. Ciovacco finds the S&P higher a week later after every correction since 1997, but the writers' bottom checklists still say "not yet." Add only after a lower low reverses on volume.

  • A seventh loss ends near 6,910S&P about -3.7%, 11.62% off its record; Nasdaq -5.3%; VIX near 35-40

    Friday: the S&P and Dow entered a correction (J.L. Bernstein, Pivot & Flow, pre-market and evening; Michael Parekh, AI Ramblings Daily #211; Alma, Stochastic Volatility). The S&P fell about 3.7% from near 7,180 to near 6,910, its seventh straight loss and the longest streak since the pandemic (Pivot & Flow, pre-market; closing level from Rob on The Hostile Takeover and Chris Ciovacco: ht-the-worst-week-of-the-year-is-the-ai-trade-doomed-20261121, cc-stocks-correction-bear-market-or-something-much-worse-20261120). Ciovacco puts the closing drawdown at 11.62% and the week's loss at more than 8%.

    It and the Dow closed at least 10% below their records, the formal definition of a correction, and the Nasdaq fell about 5.3% to turn negative for 2026 (Hostile Takeover). At midday the WSJ had it about 16% off its October high (via Parekh), and Pivot & Flow has the Nasdaq-100 close to a 20% bear-market drop.

    • Correction: an earlier version of this digest had Friday's close near 7,050 and down about 1.8%. That came from Alma's 3:50 p.m. snapshot, and the selling continued into the close.
    • The week was the S&P's worst in more than a year. Pivot & Flow's index table for Friday shows SPY -3.55%, QQQ -5.45%, DIA -1.41%, GLD -3.95% and bitcoin -5.61% on the day.
    • The close looked like capitulation: Rob has the VIX near 39.79 and Ciovacco "35 and change" (a conflict on the exact close; both are capitulation-level), and Friday had 1,199 NYSE new lows against 38 highs and 826 Nasdaq lows against 21 highs (Hostile Takeover). Rob's verdict: "Capitulation yes. Bottom not yet."
    • Ciovacco's base rate: in every correction since 1997 the S&P was higher a week later, by 3.29% on average, and in 24 secular-bull corrections 75% stopped at 15% or less. His studies aren't checkable from the wiki.
    • Bespoke (via Pivot & Flow): the S&P fell every day of the week with four big down days, a pattern seen only five other times since World War II.
    • Against the 11-19 digest: the S&P was about 8% off its high and the streak was six (market-newsletter-digest-2026-11-19 §1). Friday added another leg with no relief.
    • Why it matters a correction from a record set only on October 6 means the index has given up the whole autumn rally in about seven weeks. Jay Pelosky (Tri Polar World) says recent SPY behavior has been compared with the GFC and the Great Depression for its run of consecutive down days.
  • Thursday's internals were the worstAn 86% down day; only 36% of stocks above their 200-day; depth thinnest in a year

    Thursday's internals were the worst of the decline (Simon Ree, Tao of Trading, "What do we care about today?"). SPY opened +1.0%, peaked in the first five minutes and closed on its low, down 1.96%. QQQ fell 3.9% from open to close and broke both legs of its double bottom.

    SPY is 2.3% below its 200-day and QQQ 5.6% below.

    • Breadth: 431 liquid stocks rose and 2,457 fell, with 11% of dollar volume in advancers. Walter Deemer counts an 86% down day on Lowry's definition, the second 80%+ day of the decline after November 5's ~84%.
    • Only 36% of US stocks are above their 200-day, down from 40% Wednesday and the lowest since May 2025. At the Liberation Day low it reached about 15%, so it can get much worse. 31% are above their 50-day.
    • Market depth was the thinnest in a year: SPY moved 5.9bp for every $1B traded, 2.7x the median, and six of the twelve thinnest sessions of the past year came this month. Goldman's prime book has hedge funds net sellers of single stocks for nine straight weeks.
    • Why it matters Ree reads the joint fall in stocks, gold, silver, copper and non-US equities, with only the dollar rising, as "deleveraging rather than rotation." The test he uses: in a growth scare bonds and gold rally and credit breaks. Here gold fell about 8% over the six-day slide and junk spreads at 3.47% are only back to late March, so "Markets aren't pricing a recession."
  • Signs of tiring, before the last hourWashout checklist at 0.5 of 6; hedges not chased; Alma posted at 7,050

    The washout isn't done, but momentum is fading (Ree; Alma, "Bottom is in?" and "Status report on 20/Nov, SPX at 7050"). Ree's washout checklist is down to half a box out of six. He took back his VIX-divergence tick after the VIX closed at 28.44, and his retail-capitulation tick, because retail is still buying.

    • Vanda (via Ree): a record $111M of retail Nvidia buying in the first 30 minutes after Huang resigned, more than 40x a normal open, and Nvidia took 61% of all retail net buying that week. "Capitulation is when they stop buying." There has been no 90% down day and no volume climax: SPY traded 77.5M shares Thursday against 125.9M on November 5.
    • Price has reached his zone: on November 6 he mapped where shocks like November 5 tend to bottom and got 704-722 for SPY intraday. Thursday's low was 715.65.
    • Alma, after Friday's close: SPX made a new low, but fixed-strike vol, skew, the VIX response and the TICK low didn't confirm it. "This looks like a loss of downside momentum, rather than a reversal." TICK lows this week ran -841, -727, -697, -990 and -702: Friday's was higher than Thursday's with the index lower.
    • Her hedging data: the 6800/7000 put spread peaked at 4.04 points Tuesday, before the low, and is now 3.13 against a monthly mean of 2.82. December VIX futures rose only 1.05 points, 29% of the spot move, so backwardation widened from 3.84 to 6.38 points. "The hedges were bid ahead of the decline, but into the decline they are not being chased." She is getting short vol rather than long index delta.
    • Why it matters when hedges were bought early and aren't chased on the break, the sellers left are mostly people de-risking, not people who just discovered the risk. That is the setup for a sharp short-covering bounce, which Alma says is "not for a low."
    • Timing caveat: Alma's divergence read was posted at 3:50 p.m. with the S&P near 7,050. The last stretch took it to near 6,910 and the VIX to 35-40, so her unconfirmed-low signals may not have survived the close.
  • Dealers short gamma into expiryAbout $18.8B per 1% with the flip near 748; half rolled off Friday

Ree estimates dealers were short about $18.8B of gamma per 1% move with SPY at 715.87, up from $13.3B Tuesday, with the flip near 748 and the biggest put strike at 700. About half the short sat in Friday's monthly expiry, so it roughly halves after Friday.

    It was the 27th straight negative-gamma session.

    • SpotGamma (via Alex): calls made up about 35% of S&P delta into expiry, against a typical 50-70%.
    • Vs. prior digests this extends Ree's $13.3B figure from the 11-18 digest. The 11-19 digest's conflict, where Andreas Himmelreich said GEX stayed positive, now looks settled in Ree's favor: index moves of 2% a day with dealers chasing are short-gamma behavior.

    Global trend breaks (Ree; MacroVisor; Lead-Lag Report, "A Bear Market Hiding Inside a Bull Market"). As of Thursday the US-listed ETFs for Europe, Germany, Japan, Australia, Mexico, Taiwan and South Korea were all below their 200-day averages, six of them within six sessions. "The AI trade explains Taipei and Seoul. It doesn't explain Frankfurt, Sydney and Mexico City"; the common factor is the price of money (Ree).

TSMC is more than 40% of the local market, so the index "trades like a leveraged position" on a handful of US customers' plans.
    • Why it matters Gayed's framing is that the AI supply chain has been in a bear market since summer while the index that owns its customers had only a dip. Friday's US correction is the index starting to catch down.
  • Bear and bull readsBulltard: bottoms take weeks; Gayed's breadth case predates the drop

    The bear and bull reads (James Bulltard, "11/20/26 Recap"; Lead-Lag Report, "Equal Weight Is Beating the S&P 500"; BTIG via Pivot & Flow). Bulltard: SPY hit maximum oversold for the first time since April, but bottoms "take weeks of flattening out," and unlike April 2025 (the tariff pause) and April 2026 (the Iran ceasefire) there is no policy switch to flip. Buying tech today needs "a 3+ month minimum hold."

    • Michael Gayed's bull case: through midday November 18 the equal-weight S&P ETF was up 12.9% this year against 7.8% for SPY, the Russell 2000 ETF up 10.4% with a 10-year above 5%, and high-yield spreads at 3.43%, up from 2.60% in August but far from default pricing. "Breadth like this is what allows a hiking cycle to extend without breaking the market."
    • ConflictGayed's breadth reading predates the last three sessions. Ree has only 36% of stocks above their 200-day after Thursday, and Jeffrey Gundlach (via Pod Street Week) says nearly 60% of S&P stocks were already down more than 20% before the latest drop. Year-to-date equal-weight returns and current trend breadth are measuring different things, and the second has turned.
    • BTIG's Jonathan Krinsky (via Pivot & Flow): S&P bottoms of this size usually come with Apple well off its high, and Apple is still near its 2026 peak. He thinks chip stocks, now back where their rally started in early April, could find relief there.
    • InterArma calls five straight down days for the broad indexes an event that last happened in 2008 and 2001. Contradicted: Ree has the S&P's six-day streak as the longest since April 2024, not since 2008.

  • Five signs failed; five conditions for a reboundRob: 1,199 new lows, SMH 23M shares; DG: Anthropic above $83, junk <3.5%

Rob's bottoming checklist failed on every count: good news stopped working (Nvidia's beat sold off), bad news kept working (a denied Hunterbrook report took about 20% off AMD in two sessions), new lows grew (1,199 NYSE lows against 38 highs), down days got louder (SMH traded 23M shares against a normal 12M, closing near $400, -18% on the week) and leaders kept falling.

    "Capitulation isn't necessarily a one-day event. It can easily flow into next week."

    Defiant Gatekeeper's rebound list: Nvidia removes its own uncertainty (a CEO or timeline, a clean 10-Q), Anthropic holds $83, SpaceX gets back above $135, "most importantly, OpenAI raising its next round at or above its last valuation," hyperscalers confirm 2027 spending, the Fed signals a last hike, and junk spreads near 3.5% "must not go higher from here." Ciovacco frames it as most likely "a cyclical bear market within a secular bull," with a VIX above 33.5 historically followed by 26.9% average 12-month returns, but "oversold means nothing... until price confirms," and his firm sold more this week.

    • Defiant Gatekeeper's yield math: with fair P/E roughly 1 / (10-year + 4%), the 10-year falling from about 5.15% to 4.92% lifts it from 10.9x to 11.2x: "On the bond side, this was a good week for stocks and the S&P still lost 8.6%."     • Ciovacco's line between "mitigation one" (trimming within a secular bull) and "mitigation two" (defensive assets) is a 2000-style break, when price fell below its moving averages and stayed there; on his chart it "still looks like a strong trend period." His weekly sales were SMH, large-cap growth (VUG) and communication services (XLC), and he warns the cash "will not even under bullish conditions go back into tech."

Anthropic's Second Day: The Pop Is Gone

Investor takeaway: ANT near $86 is the entry several writers wanted, about 4% above the $83 offer, but the case still rests on growth no company its size has sustained. William Blair's $136B 2027 revenue needs about 159% growth from this year. Buy only a starter position and add after the first quarterly report shows whether growth is slowing toward Dan Niles's 25%.

  • The pop is goneLow $85.63, ~$86 late; average first-day buyer down about 16%

    Friday: most of the first-day gain gave back (Parekh; Pivot & Flow, evening; MacroVisor). ANT fell as low as $85.63 early Friday, 3.2% above the offer, and traded near $86 late, down about 10.6% on the day. Thursday it had closed at $97.17, up 17%, after opening at $111.50.

    MacroVisor noted it gave back half its opening jump Thursday and fell another 4.7% after hours.

    • Ree's math on day one: the volume-weighted average was about $102, so the average first-day buyer finished about 5% underwater even with a 17% gain on the offer. By Friday's close the average first-day buyer is down about 16%.
    • Parekh adds that a Bloomberg report on Apple building its own AI agent weighed on the stock Friday.
    • Valuation figures differ by share count: Pirate Wires put the opening valuation at $1.38T, Parekh says trading took it "to over $2 trillion," and Thursday's close was $1.76T fully diluted (The Information). Groundbreaker has the pricing at about $1.5T. These use different share bases and prices; the fully diluted figure is the one to compare with Damodaran's work in the 11-19 digest.
  • $93.5B with the greenshoe$119.5B in all; $550M of fees; run-rate revenue $81B

    The final size of the deal (Parekh, citing CNBC, Bloomberg and The Information). The underwriters exercised the greenshoe: 147M more shares at $83, adding $12.2B, for 1.127B shares and $93.5B in the IPO. With the $26B private placement, the total raised is $119.5B.

    Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley and 20 other banks earned a record $550M in fees.

    • Pivot & Flow: the $93.5B tops SpaceX's $85.7B from June as the largest IPO on record.
    • The business: run-rate revenue of $81B at October 31, up $10B in a month, and more than 11,900 customers paying over $100,000 a year (Bloomberg via Parekh). The IPO was code-named Lantern, Anthropic assembled nearly $900B of compute contracts along the way, and the founders got a special voting class. The price was raised to $83 over takeout dinner at Goldman the night before.
    • Lise Buyer (Class V Group, via The Information): "I don't think this opens the floodgates unless you look like Anthropic." That cuts against reading the deal as a reopening of the mega-IPO window for weaker names.
  • The supply drain is paid forAbout $107B absorbed vs. $4.6B-$19B of index buying

    The supply drain is paid for (Ree; Mohamed El-Erian via Pivot & Flow; MacroEdge, "Midweek Macro Note"). Ree counts about $107B of Anthropic paper the market had to absorb this week: the IPO plus the $26B placement, before any insider selling. Day-one turnover was 855M shares, about $87B, more than the $81.3B the base IPO raised.

    About 60% of it traded in the first hour.

    • Non-founder insiders and employees could sell up to 10% of their holdings from day one, as many as about 315M shares, and Ree reads the tape as many of them doing so.
    • El-Erian: investors sold other stocks to fund it, and the Nasdaq flipped from up to down right after ANT opened Thursday. "The deal is paid for now, which takes away one source of selling" (Pivot & Flow's summary).
    • MacroEdge sizes the index buying still to come. ANT joins the Nasdaq-100 at the open on December 21. Nasdaq caps a new member's weight at three times the value of its traded shares, so three times about $95B of float gives about $286B, or roughly 1.6% weight, against Jay Ritter's estimate of about 0.8%. Vanguard's own ranges: VTI 0.05-0.15%, VT 0.10-0.15%, VXF 0.65-1.30%. S&P 500 funds have to wait until at least late 2027. Published estimates of passive buying run from about $4.6B (Bloomberg Intelligence) to about $19B (Intropic).
    • Why it matters the index demand is small next to the supply. Even $19B is under a fifth of the $107B the market took this week, and the lockup schedule in the 11-19 digest is much larger.
  • Cheap at $83-86?9x 2027 revenue of $136B needs ~159% growth vs. a 1.5% base rate

    Is it cheap at $83-86? (William Blair via Pivot & Flow; Donnelly; Dan Niles via Pod Street Week). William Blair says that at $83 Anthropic trades at about 9x its estimated 2027 revenue of $136B, "very reasonable for a company growing this fast." Donnelly: "Buying ANT near the IPO price, say $84/$86 does not seem like a bad idea to me."

    • Niles is the counterweight. Anthropic's revenue grew about 144% sequentially in Q2 and 47% in Q3, and he expects closer to 25% in Q4. Open-weight models about 90% cheaper are taking share, and he thinks Copilot will route perhaps 10% of work to Anthropic. He still prefers it to OpenAI because "enterprises pay and consumers will not."
    • Base rate: $136B in 2027 from the roughly $52.5B of 2026 revenue in the 11-19 digest is about 159% growth. Companies above $50B of sales have a three-year median growth rate of 1.5% a year (base-rate-book), so the 9x multiple is cheap only if a growth rate with no precedent at this size holds.
    • Michael Kao (Kaoboy Musings) adds the liability side: Anthropic is "extrapolating exponential but slowing short-term growth into roughly $895B of long-term purchase and lease commitments Upstream," where a small demand wobble "snaps hardest."
  • Who holds it and who got richAmazon's stake $339B at $97; founders ~$22B each; Nordhaus 2.2%

    Who holds it and who got rich (SixSigma, citing Oppenheimer; Byron Gilliam, The Breakdown, "Friday charts"). Oppenheimer raised its Amazon target to $310 from $285, valuing Amazon's 3.5B Anthropic shares at $339B at $97, assuming full conversion. Amazon fell premarket Friday with ANT, so the stake now moves Amazon's price.

    • Gilliam: each of the seven co-founders is worth about $22B, and the 5,300 employees about $400B together. Each founder sold $280M of stock at the IPO price.
    • Gilliam's caution, from William Nordhaus's 2004 paper: innovators capture about 2.2% of the social surplus from innovation (about 7% at first, decaying 20% a year as competitors respond). At the 1999 peak, Nordhaus estimated, tech prices implied companies would capture 50%. "The stock market crashed in 2000... because the reality was closer to 2.2%."

AI Credit: The Swaps Are Pricing What the Ratings Aren't

Investor takeaway: Avoid the equity and debt of anyone financed against Oracle or CoreWeave leases. Groundbreaker's swap-implied marks put 72% of the owners' equity behind Oracle campuses and 91% behind CoreWeave's already gone, and loan-to-cost rose to 88% as tenant credit weakened.     The swaps have blown out (Groundbreaker, "Mark to Swap: How the Market is Mispricing the AI Boom"). Since the end of June, five-year credit default swaps are up about 155% for Broadcom, 130% for Nvidia, 80% for Oracle and 60% for Meta, and CoreWeave's has roughly doubled. The investment-grade CDX index has barely moved.

    "This is a repricing of AI credit, and of little else."
    • Converting spread to default odds (spread ÷ 60%, assuming 40% recovery): Oracle at 76bp a year ago implied about 6% five-year default odds; at 316bp it implies about 23%. CoreWeave at 1,145bp implies about 61%.
    • On Moody's market-implied grid, where investment grade ends near 135bp, every rated payer in the build trades four to seven notches below its agency rating, 5.3 on average. Microsoft (AAA) trades as A+, Meta (AA-) as BBB-, Nvidia (AA, upgraded by S&P in June) as BBB- about 6bp from high yield, and Broadcom, SpaceX and Oracle as high yield. CoreWeave (B+) trades as CCC-.     • The agencies have moved once: S&P cut Oracle to BBB- on July 9. Oracle's swap-implied rating has fallen eight notches, A to B+, in fourteen months.
  • A double-B credit financed as single-A$3.5T with lab commitments rates BB; 5.3 notches, like spring 2007

    The build as one credit (Groundbreaker). Weighting each name by its debt, leases and guarantees, the roughly $2.2T of rated obligations averages single-A at agency ratings, with about 5% in high yield and about 5% default odds. At the swaps the odds rise to about 13%, a BB+ credit with 30% below investment grade.

    Adding the labs' compute commitments (Anthropic BB and OpenAI BB- on a structural model) takes the total to about $3.5T and the rating to BB, with more than half in high yield.

    "On the market-implied ratings, the AI build is a double-B credit financed as if it were single-A."
    • The 2007 parallel: in February 2007 Moody's market-implied ratings put the big broker-dealers 4.6 notches below their agency ratings on average, 6.5 by late July. The AI build's 5.3 sits between them, "roughly where Wall Street stood in the spring of 2007." Oracle's 316bp is already wider than Bear Stearns's swap on January 1, 2008. Oracle's swap first crossed into high yield in early December 2025, eleven months ago; Bear was rescued about eight months after its own crossing, Merrill and Lehman about fourteen.
    • The circularity: about 40% of the combined backlogs of Microsoft, Oracle, Amazon and Google is owed by OpenAI and Anthropic, and those hyperscalers lend their credit back to guarantee the labs' leases. "Each side's credit is collateral for the other's."
    • Why it matters ratings decide which index a bond sits in, how much capital an insurer holds and when collateral clauses spring, so they lag by design. The swap has none of those constraints, which makes it the earliest public price of what the build's debt is worth.
  • Marking the campuses40% of owners' equity gone; 72% behind Oracle, 91% behind CoreWeave

    Marking the campuses (Groundbreaker). A leased data center is worth its lease payments, which are worth the credit behind them. Groundbreaker's cap rate: the 10-year plus a credit premium (CDS ÷ 60% × 50% building loss) plus a building premium, less rent growth.

    The model reproduces KBW's 6.7% cap rate on Core Scientific's AMD leases (single-A) and 9.2% on its CoreWeave leases (single-B) within a few basis points, and Blackstone's 7.0% purchase of AWS-leased shells. At CoreWeave's actual swap the same arithmetic gives about 16%.

Four of six CoreWeave campuses are worth less than their debt at today's swaps; with a 5.00% 10-year, so are three of the six behind Oracle.

Compute Buyers Gain Leverage: Flat Spend, Cheaper Routes

Investor takeaway: Watch Nvidia's and Broadcom's gross margins and the labs' compute costs for the first sign that buyers set the price: Google reportedly cut about 20% of next year's TPUs, OpenRouter shows spending flat for five weeks while usage climbs, and Anthropic stood up coding work on AMD in days. Beth Kindig's supply-constrained view is the counter, so don't sell memory on this alone.

  • Power shifts to the chip buyersKuo: Google cut ~20% of 2027 TPUs; Broadcom -10%+; Amodei buys late

    Power is moving to the buyers (Deirdre Bosa, Deirdre Bosa Live, Friday after the close). "My read now though is that that power is shifting away from the companies that sell AI chips and toward the big companies that buy them like Google and Anthropic." Ming-Chi Kuo reported Google cut about 20% of its TPUs from next year's plans and moved some orders to MediaTek because "Google doesn't have enough power for all the data centers that it wants"; neither company confirmed it and Broadcom fell more than 10%.

    Before the IPO, Dario Amodei told Jamie Dimon Anthropic buys less compute upfront, paying more at the last minute "only when you know that you're going to need it." An early Anthropic backer who sells on-demand compute called the boom "a GPU wastage bubble."

    Her test: "Nvidia's margins... should start to slip and Anthropic's costs should start to fall. That is when you might expect these stocks to stop moving together."

    • Why it matters chip and lab stocks have traded as one; if pricing power is moving, they split, with the chip sellers on the wrong side.
  • Usage up 13x, spending flat for five weeksOpenRouter prices -57% since January; Ramp shows the same

    Usage up, spending flat (Bosa, citing OpenRouter; Forward Guidance, citing Ramp; Dan Niles). OpenRouter: prices for Anthropic, OpenAI and Google models are down 57% since January, usage up more than 13x and spending about 6x, but for the last five weeks spending has been flat while usage kept rising, mostly on cheaper open models.

    "Usage of those open models, it's still climbing fast, but the money isn't following."

    Thompson cites Ramp the same way: "Total token usage is still skyrocketing, but if you go to the spend, it's flat." Niles has Anthropic and OpenAI's combined sequential growth slowing from about 75% to about 48% while combined operating losses rose from $7.9B to $8.4B, "because they're competing against each other on price and against open weight."

    • Data notefive weeks is a short sample, as Bosa says; Factory's Eno Reyes reads it as customers routing simpler tasks to cheaper models, which raises tokens and lowers cost per token without a fall in demand.
  • Labs can switch to AMD in daysBrown and Basis Points; Chamath: Nvidia's "over-earning era" may be over

    Buyers have options now (The Compound; Basis Points; Chamath). Brown: the labs "now have more options than they had a year ago. They can spin up an AMD server instance in a couple of days," something "none of them would have been willing to say publicly while Jensen controlled the entire planet's GPU supply." Basis Points cites Anthropic standing up coding workloads on AMD "in 3 days, not 3 months" (their claim; the source isn't named): "maybe CUDA isn't the end all be all."

    Chamath: "the over-earning era of Nvidia may be over... independent of Jensen."

  • Counter: still supply-constrainedKindig: energy and memory are the limits

    The counter: still supply-constrained (Beth Kindig, I/O Fund, Wednesday). "I am in disagreement with any idea that demand is a problem in the AI economy right now. I am 100% that we have supply constraints," with energy and memory the limits; "If there is a buyer that steps out of line, another one will come in and take that supply." Kuo's own reason for Google's cut, power, is a supply constraint, which fits her view as much as Bosa's.
