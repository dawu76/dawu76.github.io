The data come from Credit Suisse HOLT and cover the top 1,000 global companies by market capitalization from 1950 to 2015, dead companies included, with growth rates adjusted for inflation into 2015 dollars. 1. Inside View vs. Outside View

Rule of thumb: Before trusting your own model, ask what happened to companies that stood in the same spot. If your forecast beats nearly all of them, the burden of proof is on you.

~30% vs ~20%
Estimated return on the focal deal vs. comparable deals in the M&A experiment
>80%
Subjects who cut their focal-deal forecast once shown the outside view
1,000
Largest global companies each year, 1950-2015, in the book's HOLT sample
  • The job of a fundamental investor is to find a gap between the results a price implies and the results that will be revealed. Mauboussin uses pari-mutuel horse betting: the goal is not to pick the winner but to find the horse whose odds are wrong for its chance of winning.
  • The inside view is the natural way to forecast: gather information on the case, lean on your own experience, extrapolate with some adjustment. It dwells on what makes the case unique and usually produces a forecast that is too optimistic. Daniel Gilbert: "we tend to think of people as more different from one another than they actually are."
  • The outside view (reference class forecasting) asks "what happened when others were in this situation?" It relies on similarity, which is why it feels unnatural: you have to set aside your own information.
  • M&A experiment. Subjects estimated the return on a focal deal and on comparable deals. The focal deal averaged almost 30% against close to 20% for the comparables, and every subject put the focal deal at or above the comparables. When prompted with the outside view, over 80% of those with the higher focal forecast revised it down.
  • Why few people use it: it means trusting your own research less, and the reference-class statistics are often not at hand. The book exists to supply those statistics for corporate results.
  • Lovallo, Clarke and Camerer's matrix sorts how decision makers use analogies. "Single analogy" (one recalled case, full weight) over-represents the inside view and is the most common approach. The ideal is reference class forecasting: a large sample of similar cases and their full distribution.

Daniel Kahneman, quoted on the cover: "People who have information about an individual case rarely feel the need to know the statistics of the class to which the case belongs."

2. How to Blend the Two Views

Rule of thumb: Keep only r of the gap between an observed result and the average. When one year's result is mostly luck (r near 0.1), believe about a tenth of it; when it is mostly skill (r near 1), believe nearly all of it.

Estimate = grand average + shrinkage factor (≈ r) × (observed − grand average)
Fund manager after one great year
Shrinkage 0.10: the estimate of skill moves only a tenth of the way toward the observed return.
Son of a 76-inch father
Father-son height r = 0.50: the expected son lands halfway between his father and the average.
  1. Choose a reference class big enough to be robust, narrow enough to apply.
  2. Assess the distribution including skew: a few tech IPOs created most of the value.
  3. Make a prediction layer the inside view on the base rate, as a range.
  4. Check reliability regress toward the mean as far as r says.
  • Kahneman and Tversky (1973) name three inputs to a prediction: the base rate, the case specifics, and the weight on each.
  • Weight by the luck-skill continuum. Where skill dominates (a concert pianist), lean on the inside view. Where luck dominates (one year of fund returns), stay near the base rate.
  • The shrinkage formula:
    • Estimated true skill = grand average + shrinkage factor × (observed average − grand average)
    • The shrinkage factor runs from 0 (full regression to the mean) to 1.0 (none). The correlation coefficient r between two periods of the same measure is a good proxy for it.
  • Mutual fund example. Category average 8%, the fund's year 12%, shrinkage 0.10 for one-year risk-adjusted excess return: 8% + 0.10 × (12% − 8%) = 8.4%. One great year says very little about skill.
  • Heights example. Father-son height r is 0.50. A 76-inch father with a 70-inch population average implies a 73-inch son: 70 + 0.50 × (76 − 70).
  • The four steps:
    • Choose a reference class large enough to be statistically useful and narrow enough to apply.
    • Assess the distribution of outcomes. Not all are bell-shaped: of roughly 2,900 technology IPOs since 1980, a small fraction created most of the value.
    • Make a prediction, using the inside view on top of the reference-class data, as a range of probabilities.
    • Assess its reliability and regress toward the mean as far as the correlation says.

3. Regression Toward the Mean

Rule of thumb: Expect extremes to fade, at a speed set by r: with a four-year r of 0.50, half of an above-average return is gone in four years. Don't credit competition or management for a reversion that imperfect correlation produces on its own.

Microsoft: CFROI four years out

  • Whenever r between two measures of the same thing over time is below 1, results regress toward the mean. The correlation also sets the speed: high r, slow regression; low r, fast.
  • Test-score example. A student who knows 80 of 100 facts scores 90 on a random 20-question test. Skill contributed 80 and luck 10. The best guess for the next test is 80.
  • Illusion of causality. Tall fathers have tall sons, yet the tallest fathers are about 8 inches above average while the tallest sons are only about 4 inches above. The pattern also runs backward: tall sons have fathers closer to average. CFROI does the same. Quintiles sorted on 2005 CFROI converge by 2015, and quintiles sorted on 2015 CFROI also converge when traced back to 2005. "Competition" cannot run backward in time, so competition is not the whole explanation. Imperfect correlation alone produces the pattern.
  • Illusion of declining variance. Regression does not squeeze the distribution. Father and son height distributions have nearly identical coefficients of variation, and the CFROI distributions for 2005 and 2015 are nearly identical. Companies get reshuffled inside a stable distribution.
  • Use the r for the horizon you are forecasting. A consumer staples company 10 points above average CFROI fades to 6.3 points in four years if you compound the one-year r (10 × 0.89⁴), but to 7.8 points with the four-year r of 0.78. Compounding the one-year r overstates the speed of regression.
  • Microsoft worked example. CFROI 16.1%, IT sector mean 9.0%, four-year r 0.50: 9.0% + 0.50 × (16.1% − 9.0%) = 12.6% in four years. About half the excess return is gone in five years.
  • It happens on average, not to each company. Some 2005 top-quintile IT companies had even higher CFROI in 2015, and others sank below the sector average. You can depart from the regressed forecast with well-founded reasons, but regression should always be in the model because it holds for the population.

4. The Mean to Which Results Regress

Rule of thumb: Know which average you are fading toward. In stable sectors use the long-run sector mean; in cyclical ones such as energy and materials, estimate mid-cycle profitability first.

9.3% ± 0.6
Consumer staples mean CFROI and its standard deviation, 1983-2015 (CV 0.07)
4.9% ± 1.7
Energy mean CFROI and its standard deviation, 1983-2015 (CV 0.34)
  • Ask how stable the mean has been. Consumer staples CFROI averaged 9.3% from 1983 to 2015 with a standard deviation of 0.6%. Energy averaged 4.9% with a standard deviation of 1.7%. The coefficient of variation was 0.07 for staples and 0.34 for energy.
  • Low, volatile returns go with fast regression and low multiples. High, stable returns go with slow regression and high multiples.
  • Ask what drives the mean. Oil prices move energy CFROI, and regulation moves bank returns. For volatile sectors, judge where the sector is in its cycle and shade the historical average toward mid-cycle profitability. Mid-cycle profitability itself shifts if industry structure changes.
  • The US margin debate is a question about the mean. Regression of individual companies within a sector is certain. The open question is whether aggregate margins will hold, which depends on labor costs, depreciation, financing costs and tax rates.

5. Sales Growth

Rule of thumb: For any forecast of three years or more, start from the median real growth of companies the same size (about 5% for a $2-3B company, 1.5% above $50B) and argue your way off it. A plan that no company in its size class has achieved needs extraordinary evidence.

  • Sales growth is the most important driver of corporate value. Revisions to sales forecasts are usually larger than revisions to cost savings or capital efficiency.
  • Optimism. About 50% of new businesses survive five years, yet more than 8 of 10 entrepreneurs rated their own odds at 70% or higher, and one-third allowed no chance of failure.
  • Overconfidence. In one study people were right about 60% of the time on true-false questions while claiming 70% confidence. CFOs asked for 80% confidence intervals on the stock market were right only one-third of the time.
  • Analyst ranges are too narrow. Consensus three-year sales growth estimates for about 1,000 large companies had a standard deviation of 8.3%, against 18.7% for actual results since 1950.

  • Growth and its spread fall as companies get bigger. Temper growth expectations for large companies. The full-universe mean three-year CAGR is 8.1% against a 5.4% median; the distribution is skewed right, so use the median.
  • Shrinking is common. After inflation, 23% of companies had negative sales growth over three years and 20% over five years. Few analysts model a decline.
  • Persistence is low. Year-to-year sales growth r is 0.30, falling to 0.19 at three years and 0.17 at five. For forecasts of three years or more the base rate deserves most of the weight: start from the median and look for reasons to move.
  • Macro link. Median real sales growth has an r of 0.66 with US real GDP growth. GDP grew 3.2% a year from 1950 to 2015 with a 2.4% standard deviation. Corporate sales grew faster than GDP because fast growers choose to list, some activity escapes GDP, and some growth is foreign.
  • Sales growth and returns. Sales growth r with total shareholder return is 0.20 at one year, 0.25 at three and 0.28 at five. Sales are easier to forecast than earnings, but getting earnings right pays more.
  • S-curves. Color TV makers extrapolated 1960s growth and built capacity of 14 million units for peak sales of 6 million. M&A supplied about one-third of large companies' top-line growth in one study.

6. Gross Profitability

Rule of thumb: Gross profit over assets barely moves (r of 0.82 over five years), so last year's figure is a good forecast. A ratio above about 0.33 marks a quality business even when the P/E looks absurd.

~540x

0.54
Amazon gross profit / assets ($35B / $65B), above the 0.33 attractiveness line
0.54 → 0.49

  • Definition (Novy-Marx): gross profit divided by total assets. It works as a quality measure and is not positively correlated with classic value measures. Graham's late-life screen with James Rea was half valuation and half quality.
  • Highly persistent. r is 0.95 at one year, 0.89 at three and 0.82 at five. Sorted into quintiles, the spread from top to bottom shrinks only from 0.54 to 0.49 over five years. Start from last year's figure.
  • It predicts returns through portfolios, not correlations. The simple r with TSR is only 0.06 at one year, 0.18 at three and 0.24 at five. The top quintile beat the bottom quintile from 1990 to January 2016 in monthly-rebalanced portfolios of the 1,000 largest US companies, and studies of 41 countries find the same.
  • Amazon example. At year-end 2015 Amazon traded at about 540 times trailing earnings ($676 price, $1.25 EPS) against 20 for the S&P 500. Its gross profitability was 0.54 ($35 billion gross profit on $65 billion assets), well above Novy-Marx's 0.33 attractiveness threshold, and it had been above that line for most of its history. P/E and gross profitability can send opposite signals.

7. Operating Leverage

Rule of thumb: Find the fixed-cost share before forecasting earnings. In high-β sectors (materials, tech, telecom) a small sales miss becomes a large earnings miss, and debt widens it further. Growth adds value only once margins clear the threshold margin.

Same 20% margin at $10M, very different paths
Operating margin as sales change, for a cost base that is 75% fixed vs 25% fixed at $10M of sales.
75% fixed costs25% fixed costs
  • Analysts are too optimistic on earnings and miss most when a high-operating-leverage company surprises with weak sales. Buy-side analysts are more optimistic and less accurate than sell-side analysts.
  • The roadmap (from Expectations Investing): sales changes run through value factors (volume, price and mix, operating leverage, economies of scale, cost efficiencies) to operating margin, and financial leverage then amplifies the result into earnings.
  • Fixed vs. variable costs. Two businesses earn a 20% margin on $10 million of sales. With 75% fixed costs, $25 million of sales lifts the margin to 56% (the book says "nearly 60 percent"), while $5 million produces −40%. With 25% fixed costs the same moves give 32% and 0%. Theme parks run about three-quarters fixed costs, mostly labor.
  • All costs are variable in the long run, and industry growth dilutes an incumbent's fixed-cost edge.
  • Measured operating leverage ("operating margin β"). For every $1.00 change in sales, operating profit changed about $0.11 (slope 0.115 one-year, 0.104 three-year). Operating leverage is most visible in recessions and recoveries.

  • Price and mix: Goodyear. 2015 sales were 28% below 2011 and unit volume 8% lower, yet operating income rose nearly 50% and the operating margin widened 6 points as mix shifted to premium tires. Buffett calls pricing power "the single most important decision in evaluating a business." A study of about 370 goods found a 1% price change moved demand 1.76% on average.
  • Operating leverage vs. economies of scale. Operating leverage is spreading preproduction costs (a factory built for 100 widgets running at 50) over more volume. Scale economies are lower unit costs as volume grows; Home Depot's gross margin rose from 27.7% in fiscal 1996 to 29.9% in fiscal 2001 on more than $30 billion of added sales, from better supplier pricing. Mistaking the first for the second leads to the wrong belief that unit costs keep falling while capacity is added. Changes in capacity utilization and operating margin have an r of 0.60.
  • Where forecasts fail. Analyst errors are larger in high-β industries (metals) than low-β ones (food), and larger at peaks and troughs of industrial production. Analysts beat management on high-β businesses; management is better on unusual issues such as losses or excess capacity. Overall management beats analysts about half the time.
  • Financial leverage. Interest is another fixed cost. Two companies with operating profit scenarios of $80, $100 and $120: debt-free A has pretax profit from $80 to $120, a 50% range. B pays $30 of interest and gets $50 to $90, an 80% range. Companies with high operating leverage tend to carry less debt, and US cash-to-assets rose from 7% in 1980 to about 16%, consistent with R&D-heavy firms holding cash buffers.

Threshold margin (growth is not value creation)

(0.35 + 0.25) × 0.10 ÷ (1.10 × 0.65) = 0.06 ÷ 0.715 = 8.4%
  • Incremental threshold margin = (incremental fixed + working capital rate) × cost of capital ÷ [(1 + cost of capital) × (1 − tax rate)].
  • Worked example: 35% incremental fixed capital, 25% working capital, 10% cost of capital, 35% tax: (0.60 × 0.10) ÷ (1.10 × 0.65) = 0.06 ÷ 0.715 = 8.4%. A company growing 8% a year at an 8.4% margin grows sales and profit but creates no value. The more capital growth needs, the higher the margin required to break even.

Rule of thumb: Start from last year's margin and fade it gently toward the sector median; margins hold up (r of 0.72 over five years). Expect faster fade in energy, materials and tech than in consumer staples.

  • Persistent. r is 0.91 at one year, 0.79 at three and 0.72 at five. The top-to-bottom quintile spread shrinks only from 0.21 to 0.15 over ten years. Start with last year's margin.

9. Earnings Growth

Rule of thumb: Last year's earnings growth tells you almost nothing about next year's (r ≈ −0.05), so anchor multi-year EPS forecasts near the size-class median. Be most skeptical of large companies promising 15%+ a year.

  • Earnings dominate attention. Nearly two-thirds of CFOs call earnings the most important number they report, and the P/E is the most common valuation tool. Each quarterly announcement carries only 1-2% of a year's new information. About 40% of large US companies give no earnings guidance. Street EPS tracks stock prices more closely than GAAP EPS.
  • Earnings growth does not persist. Year-to-year net income growth has an r of −0.05, and the three- and five-year figures are about −0.23 to −0.24. High growers tend to swing slightly below average, and vice versa. The median deserves most of the weight for any forecast of three years or more.
  • Ranges are too narrow here too. Consensus three-year net income growth estimates had a standard deviation of 19.2% against 34.6% historically.
  • But earnings move stocks. Net income growth r with TSR is 0.20 at one year, 0.39 at three and 0.40 at five, roughly double the sales figures. Earnings are hard to predict and pay well when you get them right.
  • Base rates. The full-universe median five-year net income CAGR is 5.9% (mean 7.3%, standard deviation 20.2%); 20.3% of companies grew 10-20% a year over five years. Medians fall with size: the three-year median is 11.1% for the smallest decile and 3.6% for companies above $50 billion in sales. US after-tax corporate profits grew 3.2% a year from 1947 to 2015, the same as GDP, but with a 13.1% standard deviation against 2.6%.
  • Declines are common. 33% of companies had lower real net income year over year, 31% over three years, 29% over five and 24% over ten.
  • Alphabet example. Consensus in September 2016 implied about 15% real net income growth for three years. In the >$50 billion class, 15.4% of companies grew 10-20% over three years.

Buffett wrote in 2000 that he would bet fewer than 10 of the 200 most profitable companies would grow EPS 15% a year for 20 years, and that predicting such growth "corrode[s] CEO behavior" toward "accounting games." Mauboussin ran a version: of the 200 companies with the highest net income in 1990, 162 still existed in 2000. Fewer than 9% (14 of 162) grew net income 15% or more from 1990 to 1999, and none of those 14 did it again in the decade to 2009.

10. Cash Flow Return on Investment (CFROI)

Rule of thumb: Read the sector's four-year r as the share of an excess return that survives four years: 0.78 in consumer staples, 0.35 in energy. Pay up for high returns only where the sector record says they last.

  • CFROI is HOLT's inflation-adjusted cash return on gross investment, expressed as an internal rate of return. It removes accounting differences so companies can be compared across time, industries and countries.
  • Moderately persistent. r is 0.78 at one year and 0.56 at four. The top-to-bottom quintile spread halves from 18 to 9 percentage points over ten years.
  • Consumer sectors fade slowly; commodity sectors fast. Use the four-year r below as the shrinkage factor and the sector average as the mean.

11. Man Overboard (10%+ One-Day Drops)

Rule of thumb: After a 10%+ one-day drop, run the checklist before acting. Weak momentum going in has been a buy signal; strong momentum plus an expensive valuation has been a sell, especially on an earnings miss.

  1. Earnings or not? Pick the earnings or non-earnings table.
  2. Momentum Strong, neutral or weak going in.
  3. Valuation Cheap, neutral or expensive.
  4. Quality High, neutral or low. Read off the 30/60/90-day CAR.

  • The setup. More than 5,400 cases from January 1990 to mid-2014 of a stock falling 10% or more relative to the S&P 500 in one day. About 40% fell in the dot-com and financial-crisis periods. Outcomes are cumulative abnormal returns (CAR, beta-adjusted against the S&P 500) over the next 30, 60 and 90 trading days.
  • Why a checklist. Emotional arousal and decision quality sit on opposite ends of a seesaw. Gawande's READ-DO checklist (read the step, do the step) keeps you from deciding on emotion or freezing.
  • The READ-DO checklist:
    • Earnings or non-earnings event? Disappointing earnings releases do worse afterward than other news.
    • Momentum: strong, neutral or weak going in? (HOLT: 60% CFROI revisions over 13 weeks, 30% 52-week price momentum, 10% liquidity.)
    • Valuation: cheap, neutral or expensive? (HOLT warranted value, economic P/E, value-to-cost, dividend yield.)
    • Quality: high, neutral or low? (Last year's CFROI, managing for value, change in value creation.)
    • Scores of 66 or more are strong, cheap or high; 33 or less weak, expensive or low.
  • What the drop tables say:
    • Buy signal: weak momentum going in, strongest for non-earnings events. Weak-momentum non-earnings drops (1,867 cases) earned +11.1%, +17.0% and +18.8% CAR at 30, 60 and 90 days. Adding cheap valuation (1,008) gave +13.9%, +19.1% and +22.1%.
    • Weak-momentum earnings drops (600 cases) earned +2.7%, +5.1% and +8.4%.
    • Sell signal: strong momentum plus expensive valuation. For earnings drops, the 167 such cases averaged −4.5% CAR at 60 days. Strong momentum alone (408) gave −1.5%, −1.9% and −0.6%. For non-earnings drops the strongest sell is strong or neutral momentum with expensive valuation and high quality: 225 strong-momentum cases earned +1.9%, −2.8% and −3.2%, and 132 neutral-momentum cases −2.5%, −4.5% and −3.7%.
  • Symantec (buy). The board fired CEO Steve Bennett after the close on March 20, 2014, and the stock fell 12.9% the next day against −0.3% for the S&P 500. Non-earnings, weak momentum (23), cheap (80), high quality (71). Its reference class of 282 events averaged +10.4%, +14.9% and +23.0%. Symantec delivered +9.3%, +15.4% and +24.2%.
  • Tenet Healthcare (sell). Disappointing earnings on November 4, 2008; the stock fell 36.7% while the S&P 500 rose 4.1%, after a −25.2% CAR in the prior 30 days. Momentum barely strong (66) on its 52-week record, expensive (9), low quality (4). Reference class of 56: −2.9%, −6.3%, −1.4%. Tenet delivered −60.9%, −54.4% and −51.2%.
  • Mind the distribution. Standard deviations run 35-45%, and medians sit below means (right skew). For Symantec's class the 90-day mean was 23.0% and the median 18.2%. The base rate gives a direction, not a promise. Excluding the bubble periods, results point the same way in more than 80% of earnings branches and about 90% of non-earnings branches.

Earnings events (1,505) earned +2.7%, +3.7% and +4.1%; non-earnings events (5,292) +3.8%, +6.8% and +7.9%. Post-earnings-announcement drift is part of the story.
  • Buy signal: weak or neutral momentum going in, strengthened by cheap valuation. Weak-momentum earnings gains (665) earned +4.1%, +4.7% and +5.2%. Weak-momentum non-earnings gains (2,772) earned +5.3%, +9.1% and +10.8%, and with cheap valuation (1,270) +7.6%, +11.4% and +13.5%.
  • Sell signal: strong momentum plus expensive valuation. Non-earnings cases with both (456) averaged 0.0%, −4.5% and −6.2%; adding high quality (209) gave 0.0%, −7.0% and −7.9%.
  • Harman (buy). At an August 8, 2013 investor day Harman issued FY2014 and FY2016 guidance and rose 10.7% ($58.62 to $64.90) with the S&P 500 down 0.4%. Weak momentum (30), cheap (83), low quality (22). Reference class of 455: +7.6%, +11.0%, +12.0%. Harman delivered −1.0%, +15.5% and +10.5%.   • W.W. Grainger (sell). Strong earnings on July 18, 2012, up 11.4% with the S&P 500 up 0.7%. Strong momentum (80), expensive (19), high quality (68): "quality at any price." Reference class of 65: −2.3%, −1.7%, −6.0%. Grainger delivered −5.2%, −0.9% and −11.3%.

13. Using This in the Wiki's Own Work

Rule of thumb: Put the base rate next to every key forecast in a memo and write down why you depart from it.

  • Growth forecasts. Before accepting a company's or analyst's multi-year revenue path, look up its size decile and horizon in Section 5 and say what share of the reference class has ever done it. bayes-and-base-rates does this for OpenAI and Oracle Cloud and finds none.
  • Margin and return fade. Forecast margins and ROIC with the shrinkage formula, using the sector r and mean from Sections 8 and 10, instead of holding today's level flat. Explain any departure.
  • Scenario width. Analyst ranges run at about half the historical dispersion (8.3% vs. 18.7% for sales, 19.2% vs. 34.6% for net income). Bull and bear cases should be at least as far apart as the base-rate spread for the size class.
  • Earnings vs. sales. Sales growth is the more forecastable number; earnings growth is the one the market pays for. Treat confident multi-year EPS growth claims with the most suspicion.
  • Big one-day moves. When a holding moves 10% or more against the S&P 500 in a day, run the Section 11 or 12 checklist before acting. Sections 11 and 12 give the reference-class CARs.
  • Valuation link. A forecast that implies an outcome outside the base-rate distribution is a claim of large future value creation; see pvgo and valuations for how much of a price rests on such growth.

Caveats

  • The data stop in 2015. The rise of software and platform companies since then may have shifted the means (Section 8 already shows the top quintile pulling away).
  • Growth samples include only firms that survived the full window, a modest survivorship bias; about half of listed companies disappear within ten years, mostly through M&A.
  • All growth figures are real (inflation-adjusted to 2015 dollars). Add expected inflation before comparing with nominal guidance.
  • The drop and gain checklists rely on HOLT factor scores. Without HOLT, proxies (earnings revisions and 12-month relative price for momentum, P/E or EV/sales against sector history for valuation, ROIC over cost of capital for quality) give a rougher match.
  • Each refinement shrinks the sample. End branches in the drop and gain tables hold 23 to 282 cases, so treat small cells loosely.
