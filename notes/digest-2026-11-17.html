Nvidia's Report Night: The Setup, the Staff and the Bear Case

Investor takeaway: Don't add Nvidia on the print alone: the stock trades at its lowest forward P/E since 2013, but the multiple rests on estimates investors no longer trust, and the questions that matter are whether Kress keeps the $3-4 trillion 2030 AI spending outlook and what happens to the roughly $530B of guarantees. A break of $148.80 after earnings would open a new leg down for the AI complex.

  • The bar going in$2.47 on ~$109B; ~7.8% implied move; retail $2B in, funds $1.5B of puts

    What the Street wanted (Bernstein, both editions; MacroVisor; Wall Street Trader; Prime Report; SixSigmaCapital). Consensus was $2.47 a share on revenue of $109.15B (Bernstein), about $108.8B (MacroVisor) or about $109B (Chisca, Prime Report), against guidance near $108B. Options priced the biggest post-earnings move since February 2025, more than double the usual swing; MacroVisor put it at about 7.8% either way and Reuters, via SixSigmaCapital, at about $335B of market value.

    • Vs. prior digests market-newsletter-digest-2026-11-16 §4 had the bar at about $2.47 on about $108.7B. The estimate didn't move into the print, which means the export rule and the CEO change hadn't yet reached the numbers.
    • Positioning: retail bought a record $2B of Nvidia last week while large funds spent $1.5B on puts. Bears had cashed out puts this month, leaving few hedges below Monday's $156 close, so a miss would hit mostly unprotected holders; heavy call positions from $175 up could cap a bounce.
    • Ree's tells: a post-earnings break of Thursday's $148.80 low opens a new leg down; a reclaim of $164 says the bad news was priced. Nvidia closed Monday at $156.14 (-4.1%) on volume in the 96th percentile, its lowest close since July 1, 2025 and about 36% below its 52-week high.
    • MacroVisor's chart of the day: Nvidia at its lowest forward P/E since 2013. Ayesha Tariq wanted to hear about next year's customer commitments before calling that cheap, and expected strong numbers that wouldn't calm the market.
  • What Kress has to answer$3-4T 2030 outlook, $530B of guarantees; Huang offers to return

    The questions for Kress (Bernstein; Ree; Goodall Research). Bernstein's two: whether Kress, now interim CEO and still CFO, keeps the Huang-era forecast of $3-4 trillion of AI spending by 2030, and whether the roughly $530B Nvidia has promised or guaranteed for other companies keeps growing. Ree adds what Washington's rule does to the Southeast Asian order book.

    Nvidia is also buying $10B of Anthropic stock alongside the IPO, one more commitment Kress may be asked about.

    • Huang said in his first statement since resigning that he would return "if the Board of Directors decides that my return is in the best interest of our shareholders, our employees, the company, and America." The Journal says the board isn't considering it and has hired a search firm. Huang sits for interviews with three House committees on December 1.
    • Nvidia formed a board committee on national security policy and export compliance, warned customers that new legislation could expose them to US legal action if Chinese firms reach its chips remotely (CNBC), and filings show it cut its Intel stake by a quarter in the September quarter; the remaining $19.4B now ranks behind its SpaceX stake. Intel fell 7.02% premarket.
    • D.A. Davidson's Gil Luria cut his target from $300 to $200 and kept a Buy. Continuity: Mizuho's cut to $220 was in market-newsletter-digest-2026-11-16 §3; the targets are now converging near $200, about 28% above Monday's close.

Commerce and the Justice Department dated the Megaspeed case on Monday night: Commerce opened its investigation in 2024, asked Nvidia for its direct and indirect Megaspeed sales records in May 2025, and Nvidia put Megaspeed on its no-sell list in June 2025, the month Commerce agents and Singapore police visited the company. Megaspeed's staff kept growing until August 2025, and Bloomberg reports its Malaysian side imported at least 17,544 GB300 chips in November 2025.

The Remote-Access Rule, Read Closely

Investor takeaway: Treat Southeast Asian neocloud capacity as stranded until it finds non-Chinese tenants: the rule caps rentals to Chinese firms at about 200 Blackwells without a license, and by ChinaTalk's estimate remote access supplies two-fifths of China's AI compute. The open loophole is US soil, which favors US-sited capacity until Commerce closes it.

  • Three loopholes, one left open~200-Blackwell cap; 90 days; TSMC 14nm; US soil not covered

    What's in the 132 pages (Jordan Schneider and Lily Ottinger, ChinaTalk, with Chris McGuire and Julian Gewirtz). McGuire sorts the rule into three parts. First, licenses for advanced chips shipped to Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil; relative to GDP, the first four were the 3rd, 4th, 5th and 8th largest AI chip importers in the world, and Brazil hosts ByteDance's builds.

    Rental cap to Chinese firms without a license~200 Blackwells or ~800 H100s

    TSMC routeLicense for any Chinese chip at 14nm or better

    Chris McGuire on ChinaTalk. Huawei made about 3 million AI chips at TSMC, ~$45B of product.

    Second, a cap on compute rented to Chinese companies: roughly 200 top Blackwells or about 800 H100s without a license. Third, closing the TSMC route for Chinese chip designers.

    • The rental cap applies retroactively in effect: providing at least half of your compute to Chinese companies "was itself a violation," which could reach Megaspeed if enforced. After 90 days, providers can serve Chinese clients only for recommendation engines, a carve-out McGuire says could become "a loophole you can drive a Mack truck through" if read broadly.
    • The US loophole: the rule doesn't apply inside the United States, so "Northern Virginia is going to become the center of China AI until we fix this." McGuire says Commerce can close it now.
    • TSMC: any Chinese company making a chip at 14nm or better now needs a license whether or not it says the chip is for AI, so Xiaomi needs one for a 3nm phone chip. McGuire says Huawei got about 3 million AI chips made at TSMC, roughly $45B of product at $15,000 each, through a loophole the Biden administration closed, the Trump administration reopened, and Monday's rule closed again.
    • Vs. prior digests china-ai-compute-access-export-controls-2026 estimated the US-soil gap and the 90-day window for existing capacity (to about February 17, 2027); ChinaTalk's reading matches both.
  • Labs call to rent the stranded capacityRemote access ~two-fifths of China compute; BNP $9B vs. AMAT $500M

    Who absorbs the compute (ChinaTalk). Schneider puts about two-fifths of China's total compute in remote access, based on ChinaTalk's April estimate. McGuire says several AI labs called him Monday morning asking who is most affected so they could rent the stranded capacity, and that "export controls have not negatively impacted a single Nvidia sale so far," because Nvidia has sold every chip it made.

    • His reading of why Nvidia sold into the region anyway: the Journal's story of Huang telling neocloud CEOs about a Japanese soup vendor who refused to sell a second bowl, keeping prices high and customers diverse. Few companies can buy $100-200B of chips a year, and outside the US hyperscalers the only customers at that scale are in China or the Middle East.
    • Enforcement is the open question. McGuire contrasts BNP Paribas's $9B sanctions fine, which changed bank behavior, with Applied Materials' $500M export fine, which he calls a drop in the bucket next to its China sales. Gewirtz notes the rule is a licensing regime, so Beijing may work to soften it in implementation rather than retaliate.
  • Legal by designMalaysia $34B; DayOne ~70% ByteDance; China 7% reported vs. Culper 20%+

    Legal by design (Michael Parekh, AI-RTZ #1273). Parekh separates three routes: licensed direct exports, remote rental outside China (legal until Monday), and smuggling (illegal and prosecuted).
