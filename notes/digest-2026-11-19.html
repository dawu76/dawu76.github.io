The Tape: A Sixth Straight Loss and a Nasdaq Correction

Investor takeaway: Hold off on new equity buying until QQQ and SMH hold their double bottoms. The S&P fell about 2% to near 7,180, its sixth straight loss, with decliners ahead 10:1. A reclaim of the 200-day that faded into a bearish engulfing candle isn't a bottom, even with NAAIM down to 51.

  • A sixth straight lossS&P down about 2% to near 7,180, 8% off its high; 93% red; VIX 29, highest since March

    Thursday: the Anthropic open couldn't lift the market (Brian Szytel, The Bahnsen Group, Dividend Cafe; David Marlin, Marlin Capital; J.L. Bernstein, Pivot & Flow, evening edition). The S&P fell about 2% to near 7,180, its sixth straight loss, and the Dow and Nasdaq each fell a little over 2%. Decliners outpaced advancers 10:1 (Bahnsen) and about 93% of S&P stocks closed red (Marlin).

    The Nasdaq gave up an early 2% gain, and the Dow closed below 50,000.

    • Size of the drawdown: the S&P is about 8% off its high and down about 5% this week (Marlin), up from about 6.5% off the high in the 11-18 digest (market-newsletter-digest-2026-11-18 §1).
    • The VIX hit 29, its highest since March (Marlin), above the November 5 peak of 28 (Sharpe Two), so fear has now confirmed the lower low that Wednesday's tape lacked.
    • Pivot & Flow: all 11 S&P sectors fell and the Nasdaq is in a correction (down more than 10% from its peak). Energy held up best, down less than 1%, and consumer discretionary was worst, down about 3%.
    • Why it matters on November 5 a broad selloff came with a single-day 4.4% drop. This one is a grind: six losses, with the worst day at about 2%. Grinding declines with breadth this bad (93% red) usually mean forced or patient selling rather than a single shock.
  • Wednesday, gradedSPY about 6% under its October high, first close below the 200-day in 155 sessions

Wednesday the S&P dipped under Alma's line intraday and closed back above it, "so from the SPX side is the bearish signal not confirmed yet." Only the Dow closed under its October low.

    • Thursday morning's bounce stalled just under Alma's 7,400-7,425 resistance zone, and the cash session failed from there.
    • Sharpe Two: after five straight losses, the longest streak since June, SPY closed Wednesday about 6% below its October 6 high and under the November 5 selloff low. It was the first close below the 200-day in 155 sessions.
    • Realized volatility: 30-day realized is 16.0%, at the 80th percentile of two years against a median of 12.9%, with nine-day at 17.6%. Its model sees 30-day realized rising toward 20%. Sharpe Two still thinks options overprice the next few weeks: "a busy month for SPY, but nowhere near a broken one."
  • Flows washed out, not finishedGoldman Prime 4+ sigma tech selling; NAAIM 51; MacroEdge -8.55% in five sessions

Marlin cites Goldman Prime: hedge funds have sold tech for six weeks in a 4+ sigma move, something that "hasn't even come close to happening in 10 years of data." Most of his readers took it as bullish.

    • Alex took exposure back up to about 63% Wednesday on "constructive decoupling": AMD, AMZN, AVGO and SPCX reclaimed their 21-day averages while QQQ stayed weak. He was clear it was "not me calling a bottom." Thursday's broad drop tested those reclaims the next day.
    • Vanda Research (via Pivot & Flow): retail traders sold other AI stocks to pay for Anthropic, and AI cloud names like Nebius and CoreWeave took the worst of it. MacroEdge had expected the IPO's cash drain to end once the stock went live. Thursday it didn't.

  • The bull read: dealers long gammaGEX positive, VIXY stretched; conflicts with Ree's $13.3B short gamma

On his read market makers are long gamma and buying dips, VIXY is "stretched at the top of its range (ripe for a vol crush)" and DIX shows a bullish divergence.

    QQQ and SMH both printed double bottoms, and high-beta AI leaders like COHR are making higher lows.

    • His logic: a VIXY spike normally means dealer panic and negative gamma, but with GEX positive it "reflects overcrowded, overpriced event hedges." Once those unwind after the IPO, "the resulting vol crush forces dealers and funds to chase high-beta leaders higher."

Thursday's 2% drop with the VIX at 29 looks more like Ree's short-gamma market than a dampened one.
  • 1998, 2000 or 2023?Compute operators nearly 50% down in a quarter; watch credit, orders and funding

    Which crash is this? (Reader: "Not easy being in the market right now" (Pepe Maltese) https://twitter.com/pepe_maltese/status/2123240362290389158). Pepe Maltese compares the clusters with three earlier markets:

    • 1998: forced deleveraging followed by a tech melt-up.
    • 2000: an infrastructure overbuild where "Wall St suddenly remembers that capital has a cost."
    • 2022-23: rates crush multiples until a few earners break away.
    • Now: AI semis and optical are down about 25% over the month and compute operators nearly 50% over the quarter, while "financial tollbooths, selected software and healthcare are quietly making money." "The market isnt rejecting growth, its rejecting growth that needs another few billion dollars befote the cash starts arriving."
    • If credit normalizes and AI demand holds, $MRVL, $MXL, $COHR and $IREN "could have one hell of a second leg." If orders vanish, it's 2000. His third case is "2023 on steroids," where only firms with pricing power and customer financing grow through expensive capital.
    • The test he offers: "a 20% rebound doesnt tell you whether the liquidation is over." Watch credit, actual orders and who gets funded. That gives a checklist for telling a 1998 flush from a 2000 bust, instead of reading the bounce itself.
  • 200 S&P names moving against the indexLundy: ~40% inversely correlated in September; Brey: dispersion unwinding

    Liquidity, dispersion and a record of stocks moving against the index (Brad Lundy, Butler Hall Capital, on Benny & The Squirrel; Ben Brey with George Gammon; Andreas Steno with Raoul Pal, taped about November 11-12). Lundy cites Goldman data that about 200 S&P names moved inversely to the index in September, about 40%, against about 15% in 1999-2000 and above 20% in Q2: "there has never been a period where 200 names in the S&P are inversely correlated" (his citation, not checked). He reads it as an opportunity for uncorrelated holdings and "probably an omen."

    Brey's mechanism: multi-manager pods running market-neutral books pushed "single stock vol... like 99th percentile" while "implied correlation" sat near the bottom, and the last crowded trade "they're coming for is the dispersion trade," long single-stock vol and short index vol.

    "What you're really short is liquidity... if the price of volatility goes up, price of money goes up, deleveraging happens."

    With passive at "50 to 55% of the market" (citing Mike Green), the market is "inelastic" with "air pockets."

    • Steno's version, taped before Huang resigned: long-short and multi-strategy funds ran "very very close to their max capacity" through October and cut risk on any headline, and he says "more than a hundred" macro PMs lost their seats that month (his count). Leverage now hides in leveraged ETFs and retail options rather than margin debt. Pal: "risk on risk off is just a function of liquidity."
    • Why it matters all three describe a selloff driven by who holds stocks and how they're funded rather than by earnings, which fits Himmelreich's dealer-gamma read (above) and argues for sizing so you can hold through air pockets; Lundy caps each long's loss at about 150 bp.

Anthropic's First Day: $83, Then $111.50, Then $97.17

Investor takeaway: Don't chase ANT here. It closed at $97.17, 17% above the $83 offer but 13% below the $111.50 open. The next supply is already scheduled: 147M greenshoe shares by Friday, options from Monday, 315M employee shares now and a $305B–$789B lockup cliff in 3-6 months. The first forced buying is the Nasdaq-100 add after Dec. 18.

  • The pricing980M shares at $83 for $81.3B; more than $107B with the placement; Kantrowitz's 80M is a typo

    The pricing (J.L. Bernstein, Pivot & Flow; Michael Parekh, AI Ramblings Daily #210; Matt Levine, Money Stuff; Alex Kantrowitz, Big Technology). Anthropic sold 980M shares at $83, above the original $70-78 range, raising $81.3B, the largest IPO on record. Counting the $26B private placement from Google, Microsoft, Nvidia and AMD, the total is more than $107B (Parekh, citing Bloomberg; Levine has "about $107B").

    • Vs. prior digests the 11-18 digest had 825M shares marketed at $70-78 for $90.4B at the top including the placement, at about $1.4T fully diluted (market-newsletter-digest-2026-11-18 §6). Pricing at $83 and upsizing by 155M shares added about $17B to the raise. Levine and The Prime Report now put the fully diluted value at about $1.5T at the offer price.
    • Levine explains the ceiling: SEC rules don't let an issuer raise an IPO's size or price by more than about 20% without revising the prospectus. 825M to 980M is about a 19% increase, so the bankers went close to the limit.
    • ConflictKantrowitz writes "selling 80,000,000 shares at $83 a piece" for "a staggering $81 billion." 80M shares at $83 is about $6.6B, so his share count is missing a digit. Pivot & Flow, Parekh and Levine all have 980M.
    • Pivot & Flow, pre-market: seven tech companies bought $26B of stock at $83, and five are chipmakers, Nvidia and TSMC among them, "that count on Anthropic's roughly $900 billion in spending commitments." "So some of the money behind today's chip rally is the chipmakers' own." That is the circular-financing pattern from the Nvidia pages, now running into the lab's own equity.
  • The open and the fade$111.50 open (+34%), $97.17 close; the perps had it near $95

    The open and the fade (Levine; Byron Gilliam, The Breakdown; Marlin; Szytel; Newcomer; Pivot & Flow). Shares began trading at 11:48:24, two hours and 18 minutes after the market opened (Gilliam). About 108M shares crossed in the opening auction at $111.50, a 34% pop (Levine).

    Reuters (via Parekh) put that at about $2T, counting every option and convertible share.

    • It then "slid back to around $97" (Pivot & Flow). Levine had $95.79 at about 2 pm, about 15% over the offer. Marlin has the close at $97.17. Szytel: it "gave back about half" of the pop.
    • Yahoo Finance (via Pivot & Flow): no big US IPO since 2008 fell this far from its first trade in the first half hour.
    • Newcomer's "popped 30% at the opening" for "briefly... $2 trillion" is a rounder version of the same $111.50 print.
    • Levine says the bankers got "pretty close" to the traditional target pop of about 20%. Gilliam's point is about the perps: "The quick move back to $95 is the stock returning to where the perps market was for most of this week," so "the perps market on Tuesday was more accurate than Nasdaq this morning." The 11-18 digest had perps around $1.7T (market-newsletter-digest-2026-11-18 §6). The close sits much nearer that than the open does.
    • Ayesha Tariq (MacroVisor) had gray-market indications of a 22-26% gain before the open. The close at 17% came in under that range, after an open well above it.
  • Zeroed bidders bought the openMore than half of about 1,200 institutions got nothing; ~$500B of orders

    Allocation: zeroed bidders bought the open (Levine; Parekh). More than half of about 1,200 institutional bidders got no shares, and allocations skewed to US investors (Bloomberg via Levine). European retail took $1.1B and Japan raised $3B, at the low end of its range (Parekh, citing Bloomberg).

    • Levine's mechanism: some of the roughly $500B of demand "for about $100B of stock" was inflated by design. But "a lot of the unmet demand was real," and zeroed bidders "came back to the market today to buy what they needed." "Anthropic's banks didn't just line up buyers for the IPO; they also lined up buyers for the post-IPO trading today."
    • That explains the shape of the day. The open was set by forced catch-up buying from zeroed accounts, and the fade came once that bid was filled. The open price says little about where long-term holders value the stock.
    • Levine on the "must own" logic (citing Craig Coben in the FT): many investors had reservations about the valuation, the capex and commoditization, "But, they kept saying, the stock is a must-own." For an index-relative manager, being underweight is a risk too. Nir Kaissar's counterpoint: "It's all right to pass."

What Anthropic Is Worth

Investor takeaway: At $97.17 the stock is about 26% above Damodaran's option-adjusted value of $76.98 and about 30x this year's roughly $52.5B of revenue. Owning it means betting on about 161% revenue growth next year. Companies above $50B of sales have a median growth rate of 1.5% a year, so size the position as an option rather than a core holding.

  • Damodaran: $76.98 a shareRange $76.46 to $90.86; the close is about 26% above

    Damodaran's per-share value: $76.98 (Aswath Damodaran, Musings on Markets, "From Aggregate Equity Value to Value per share"). He cut his aggregate equity value to $1,351,556 million from $1.475T on November 2. Four changes drove it:

    • Third-quarter base-year numbers.
    • Cash proceeds of about $60B rather than the rumored $100B.
    • Post-midterm data-center costs: sales per dollar of capital in the first five years cut to $2.75 from $3.
    • A larger regulatory drag on margins, 6% from 5%, which lowers the target margin to 26.5%.
    • The share count ranges from 14,875.18M (shares outstanding only) to 17,676.18M fully diluted. It includes 801M new IPO shares, 1,026M unvested restricted shares, 1,774M options at a $2.91 strike (1,082M vested) and 933.7M shares reserved for the donation-matching plan.

    The moat debate (Reader: "No, Anthropic Won't Be the Only Company Left" (Christian Catalini) https://twitter.com/ccatalini/status/2123426576197149105; Reader: "My biggest hot take right now..." His history:

    • Railroads: in 1890 they earned an estimated 3.5% private return against a 48% social return. More than 100 railway companies went into receivership after the Panic of 1893, and without the rails US farmland would have been worth 60% less.
    • Cars and gasoline: more than 700 US automakers shrank to the Big Three by 1941. Gasoline went from a near-worthless byproduct to 44% of crude processed in 1931.
    • Electricity: motors went from under 5% of factory drive in 1899 to almost 80% three decades later, and manufacturing productivity growth rose from 1.5% to 5.1% a year.
    • Sears rode the rails from $750,000 of sales in 1895 to $50M in 1907.

Demand splits into commodity tokens "metered like kilowatt-hours" and value-added ones. "Anthropic may well become one of the world's most valuable companies. But it will never be the only one."

He also says switching costs are "near 0." Sonne says he holds no positions. His points line up with Catalini's: a frontier lead that is hard to protect.

Theme 4

The Build-Out Math: Supply Compounds, Demand Comes in Steps

Investor takeaway: Size AI-infrastructure positions for a 2028-2029 glut, not a 2027 one. At this year's pace of about $10B a month of new lab ARR, revenue covers about 80% of what the 2028 fleet needs and 68% of 2029's, so the trade needs the labs to reach $15-20B a month. Watch the November and December ARR prints, not chip orders.

    • At this year's pace, AI revenue covers about 80% of what the consensus fleet needs in 2028 and 68% in 2029. That leaves about 15 GW unsupported in 2028 and 40 GW in 2029: "a glut as early as 2028."
    • At $20B a month the world is short capacity, and the bull case needs upward of 60 GW a year. His base case of $15B a month sits on the line in 2028 and slides into a glut in 2029. All of these assume every revenue dollar pays for compute with no margin, which flatters demand.
    • The mechanism: "Supply compounds," because gigawatts keep landing and each does more work every year. "Demand doesn't grow this way. It moves in steps as new TAMs unlock" (chat in 2023, reasoning in late 2024, coding and agents since). "Each unlock opens a shortage, which supply then closes and overshoots until the next unlock."
    • Why the next two months matter: "changing your heading by a couple of degrees puts you miles apart down the road." November and December show whether 2027 starts closer to $10B or $20B a month.

      • NAND pricing is expected to fall in Q1 and Q2 2027.

    • Its model is Buffett during the 1963 salad-oil scandal. American Express took on $60M of liabilities on about $175M of fake soybean oil, and the stock fell about 45% by June 1964. Buffett tracked one KPI, card usage, put about 40% of his fund in and made about 5x by 1968.

    • Thornton backtested two retirees with $500,000 from 2007 drawing $20,000 a year, inflation-adjusted, $488,041 in total. The one who sold shares ended with $740,408 ($148,082 in stocks) after selling $199,060. The one who drew dividends, put and call premium and T-bill interest first, $403,197 of income, sold only $102,742 and ended with $1,208,180, $966,544 of it in stocks.
    • The costs: $84,172 of gains given up to calls and a 33% drawdown in 2008 against 24%. The same stocks without options had to sell $208,916, so the options did the work. In a taxable account the cut in selling shrank to 18%, and across start years 2007-2016 it ranged from 48% to 91%. The result rests on a 2007-2026 run that was good for stocks, which helped the strategy that stayed invested.
